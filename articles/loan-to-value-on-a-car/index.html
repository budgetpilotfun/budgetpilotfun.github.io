Borrowing

Loan-to-value on a car loan

Divide what you owe by what the car would sell for. That single percentage says more about your position than the monthly payment ever will, and it moves every month whether you watch it or not.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Cushion, under 80% Thin, 80 to 100% Underwater, 100 to 125% Deep, 125% and up

The illustrative loan from this page, on the day it was signed.

That needle is the whole subject. Everything below is about how it gets placed, what the colored bands mean, and what pushes it left or right.

First, the bands. They are our own reading aid, drawn at round numbers so the meter has something to say. They are not any lender's cutoffs. Lenders do look at loan-to-value when they decide how much to lend against a car, and each one draws its own lines; if you want to know where a particular lender draws them, that is a question for the lender. For your own purposes, the bands answer a simpler question: if the car had to be sold or was written off tomorrow, would the money cover the loan?

Cushion means yes, with room to spare. Thin means yes, barely, and a bad dent or a soft market could erase it. Underwater means no: selling the car leaves a balance you still owe. Deep means that balance is large enough to steer your next several decisions.

loan-to-value = loan balance ÷ current market value × 100

Both inputs deserve a second look. The loan balance you want is the payoff amount, not the principal printed on last month's statement; the payoff includes interest accrued since then. The market value you want is what the car would actually fetch, which is usually closer to a dealer's trade-in offer than to an asking price on a listing site. Use the honest version of each and the needle lands honestly.

Placing the needle, with one illustrative loan

Take a car priced at $25,000. The buyer puts nothing down and finances $2,000 of taxes and fees along with it, for a loan of $27,000 over 60 months at an illustrative 7% APR. The payment works out to about $534.63 a month.

On day one, suppose the car's market value is the full $25,000. That is generous, since a car usually loses some value the moment it becomes used, but it keeps the example clean. $27,000 ÷ $25,000 × 100 = 108%. The buyer is underwater before the first payment, purely because the fees were borrowed rather than paid.

Now the same purchase with $5,000 down and the fees paid in cash: the loan is $22,000. $22,000 ÷ $25,000 × 100 = 88%. Still in the thin band, but on the right side of 100.

The zero-down version is the one worth watching over time. Below, its balance follows the actual amortization of that loan. The car values are illustrative assumptions about depreciation, not a forecast for any real model; your car will follow its own curve.

Month 12. Balance about $22,326; assumed value $21,000. The loan has shrunk by roughly $4,700, but the car has lost $4,000, so the needle has barely moved.

Month 24. Balance about $17,315; assumed value $18,000. The needle crosses back over 100 somewhere in the second year.

Month 36. Balance about $11,941; assumed value $15,500. Now there is a cushion, and it grows from here.

The shape is typical of any loan that starts above 100%: early payments are interest-heavy, early depreciation is steep, and the two roughly cancel for a while. The needle sits still, then starts to swing left once the payments tilt toward principal and the car's value loss slows. How long the stall lasts depends on the rate, the term and the car. A longer term makes it last longer, because each payment retires less principal. How an auto loan payment is built shows the interest-to-principal shift in detail.

What moves the needle

Left, toward safety: a down payment; paying taxes and fees in cash rather than financing them; a shorter term; extra payments toward principal; a car that holds its value well.

Right, toward trouble: financed add-ons such as service contracts and accessories; negative equity carried over from a previous car; a long term; an accident on the history report, which lowers what the car will fetch; a soft used-car market, which lowers everything.

Rolling old negative equity into a new loan is the fastest way to start deep in the red zone. If the old car was $4,000 underwater, that $4,000 joins the new loan on day one and the new car has to depreciate on top of it. Rolling negative equity into the next loan follows that staircase across three cars.

Why the number matters on an ordinary Tuesday

Most weeks, it doesn't. You make the payment, you drive the car, and nobody asks. Loan-to-value only bites when something forces the car to become money: a total-loss accident, a sudden need to sell, a job move, a change in the household. On those days, the needle is the difference between walking away clean and writing a check for a car you no longer have.

That is also the case for knowing your number in advance. If you are underwater and the car is written off, insurance generally pays the car's actual cash value, not your balance; the gap between them is yours unless you carry gap coverage. If you are underwater and want to sell, the buyer's money won't clear the lien on its own. Neither situation is a crisis if you saw it coming.

Checking takes ten minutes. Ask your lender for a payoff quote. Get a trade-in figure or two for your car's year, mileage and condition. Divide one by the other. If the needle is left of 100, carry on. If it is right of 100, the practical responses are all the same kind of thing: avoid adding to the balance, keep the car longer, and consider sending a little extra toward principal when the budget allows.
