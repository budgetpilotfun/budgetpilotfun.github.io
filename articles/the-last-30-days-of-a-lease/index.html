Leasing

The last 30 days of a lease

Find the maturity date on your contract and count back thirty days. That is day 30. Everything below hangs off it.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Timelines here are suggestions, not deadlines from any contract. Some lessors ask for notice or an inspection further ahead; your agreement and the lessor’s end-of-term letter set the real dates. If they say sixty days, start this list earlier.

Day30

Day 30: decide which way you’re going

  • Choose, at least provisionally: return it, buy it, or trade it toward something else.

    Every other item on this list depends on that choice. Your options at lease end lays out what follows each one.

  • Pull out the agreement and note the maturity date, the mileage allowance, the per-mile charge, the purchase option price and any end-of-term fee.

    You want those five facts in one place before any phone call.

  • Photograph the odometer.

    It tells you whether the miles are a problem while there’s still time to drive less.

Day28

Day 28: get the payoff number

  • Call the lessor for a payoff quote, even if you plan to return the car.

    It’s the only way to know whether the car is worth more than you’d pay for it. The call to your leasing company lists what to ask.

  • Look up what comparable cars are selling for.

    If the market value is comfortably above the payoff, returning it hands that difference back to the lessor.

Day21

Day 21: look at the car the way an inspector will

  • Ask whether the lessor offers a pre-return inspection, and book it if so.

    A written list of what they’d charge for, three weeks out, is far more useful than a surprise after the fact.

  • Walk the car in daylight: every panel, each wheel, the glass, the seats, the headliner.

    The contract’s wear standard decides what counts; the guide to wear and tear shows how zones are usually judged.

  • Check tire tread and whether all four tires match the lease’s requirements, if it has any.

    Tires are a common line on turn-in reports and easy to price in advance.

Day14

Day 14: fix, or accept the charge

  • For each flagged item, get a repair price and compare it with what the lessor would bill.

    Sometimes a small independent repair is cheaper; sometimes paying the charge is. Only the two numbers side by side will tell you.

  • Book any repairs now, not the week of turn-in.

    Shops run late. Parts run later.

  • If buying or trading, line up the money: savings, a loan, or the trade-in deal.

    Funds and paperwork take longer than people expect, and payoff quotes expire.

Day10

Day 10: gather what goes back with it

  • Find every key and fob, the owner’s manual, and any accessories that came with the car.

    Missing keys and parts can be billed. A replacement fob is not a cheap thing to discover you owe.

  • Pull the service records into one folder.

    Useful if maintenance is questioned, and for a buyer if you’re trading.

Day7

Day 7: book the handover

  • Confirm where the car is to be returned and make an appointment if the location needs one.

    Not every dealership accepts every lessor’s returns, and walk-ins aren’t always welcome.

  • Arrange how you’ll get home.

    Small, and routinely forgotten.

Day5

Day 5: settle the running account

  • Log in to the lessor’s account and confirm the last scheduled payment has gone through, and whether any payment falls due after the maturity date.

    A final monthly payment that bounces or was never scheduled becomes a late charge on the closing statement.

  • Turn off autopay only if the lessor confirms nothing further will be drawn through it.

    Cancelling it too early is a common way to miss the very last payment.

  • Estimate the final odometer reading and, if you’re over the allowance, multiply the excess by the contract’s per-mile rate.

    Knowing the figure now lets you set the cash aside rather than meet it cold on the statement.

Day3

Day 3: empty it and wipe it

  • Remove toll transponders, parking permits, garage openers, charging cables you own, child seats.

    A transponder left behind can keep billing your account.

  • Unpair your phone, erase saved addresses and contacts, and sign out of any connected-car app or subscription tied to the vehicle.

    The car’s next driver shouldn’t have your home address a tap away.

  • Clean it inside and out.

    Dirt can hide damage from you and exaggerate it to an inspector.

Day1

Day 1: make your own record

  • Photograph and video every side, each wheel, the windshield, the interior and the odometer, in good light.

    If a charge appears later, your dated files are the only independent evidence of the car’s condition on the day.

  • Confirm your insurer knows the policy is ending soon, but leave the coverage active.

    You are responsible for the car until someone signs for it.

Day0

Day 0: hand it over, and get paper

  • Get a signed return receipt showing the date, time and odometer reading. Keep a copy.

    It is the document that ends your responsibility for the car.

  • Ask whether the inspection happens on the spot or later, and whether you’ll get a copy of the report.

    You’ll want to compare it against your photos.

  • Deal with the plates as your state’s motor vehicle agency requires.

    Some states want plates returned, some let you transfer them, some neither. Check before you unscrew anything.

  • Only after the receipt is in hand, cancel or move the insurance.

    Not a day before.

After day 0. The lease isn’t quite over when you walk away. A final statement usually follows within weeks, listing any end-of-term fee, excess wear, excess miles, and remaining amounts owed or credits due. Read each line against the contract and your photos. Set aside the money for it at day 30, and this last envelope won’t sting.
