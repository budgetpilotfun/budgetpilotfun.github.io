What your insurer pays when your car is totaled

The check is not for what you paid, and it is not for what you owe. It is for a figure called actual cash value, assembled from a handful of choices someone made about your car. Here are the choices, one at a time.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Many policies pay a totaled car at its actual cash value: roughly, what the car was worth immediately before the loss, in its condition, in your market. Your policy defines the term, and the definition in your own declarations and policy form is the one that counts.

Below, one illustrative valuation is built from scratch. The car, the comparables and every dollar figure are invented to show the mechanics; they don’t reflect any insurer’s method or any real vehicle’s value. Follow the running figure down the right-hand side.

That last row is the one people don’t see coming. A settlement is based on the car’s value; a loan balance is based on what was borrowed and how much has been repaid. When the two drift apart, the gap is yours. Whether gap coverage makes sense, and when to drop it, is worked through in gap coverage, and when it earns its keep.

Why it was totaled in the first place

A car is declared a total loss when repairing it no longer makes economic sense against its value. How that line is drawn depends on the insurer and, in some states, on a threshold set by law; it usually weighs the repair estimate against the actual cash value, and sometimes what the wreck would fetch as salvage. That is why an older car can be totaled by damage that would be repaired without a second thought on a newer one. The repair bill didn’t grow; the value it is measured against shrank.

It also means the ACV does double duty. It decides whether the car is totaled and how much you receive. A valuation that runs low can tip a repairable car into a total loss and then pay less for it.

Some owners ask to keep the totaled car. Where that’s allowed, the insurer typically deducts a salvage value from the settlement, and the car may then carry a branded title that affects its resale and insurability. Ask your insurer, and the state office that issues titles, before choosing it.

Where the number can be questioned

Every adjustment above was a judgment, and judgments can be challenged with evidence. Ask for the full valuation report in writing before you respond to an offer. Then go through it looking for these:

  1. Comparables that aren’t comparable. A lower trim level, a different drivetrain, a car from a market far from yours, or an asking price treated as a sale price. Find your own comps from listings near you and send them.
  2. The mileage adjustment rate. Check both the mile difference and the per-mile figure. A small rate applied to the wrong baseline adds up.
  3. The condition rating. If the adjuster rated the car below how it actually was, send photos taken before the loss, service records and inspection reports.
  4. Missing equipment and recent investments. Factory options left off the report, and receipts for recent work such as new tires or a new battery. Whether recent maintenance raises value varies, but it costs nothing to submit it.
  5. Taxes and fees. If the settlement leaves them out, ask what your policy and state require.
  6. The appraisal clause. Many policies include a process for resolving a disagreement over value, in which each side hires an appraiser. Read yours for the steps and the costs you would bear.

If the conversation stalls, every state has a department of insurance that accepts consumer complaints; its website will describe the process. Most disputes never get that far. A well-documented counteroffer, with comps and receipts attached, is often where they end.

Keep notes of every call, with dates and names. The hour-by-hour claim log shows one way to keep them. Then, before accepting, reread the settlement letter once more with the loan statement beside it.
