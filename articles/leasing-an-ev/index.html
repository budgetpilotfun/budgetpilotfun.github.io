Leasing

Leasing vs buying an electric car

A formal debate, held on paper. One side argues for leasing, the other for buying, each gets a reply, and a referee has the last word.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read
The motion

“This house would lease its next electric car rather than buy it.”

The two cases below are argued as strongly as each side can honestly argue them. Neither is a recommendation. Figures are illustrative, and incentive rules are deliberately left out of both cases: they have changed often, they can treat a leased car differently from a purchased one, and anything written here could be out of date by the time you read it. Check fueleconomy.gov and irs.gov for what applies to you now.

Proposition

The case for leasing

  1. Nobody knows what this car will be worth in three years. Resale values for electric cars depend on things that move quickly: battery chemistry, charging speed, charging standards, the price of the next model year, incentive changes. A lease writes the future value into the contract on day one. If the market lands below that number, the lessee hands the car back and the lessor absorbs the difference. That is insurance against the single largest cost of owning a car, and it comes bundled with the lease. Residual value is the mechanism.
  2. The technology is still moving. Range, charging speed and software have changed noticeably between model generations. A three-year lease lets you step to whatever is current without first selling a car whose value the market is still arguing about.
  3. Battery health stops being your question. An owner who sells at year eight is selling a battery of uncertain condition to a buyer who knows it. A lessee returns the car within the lease’s wear terms and moves on.
  4. The door stays open. Most leases include a purchase option. If the car turns out to be worth more than that price at the end, the lessee can buy it and keep the upside. The lessee gets the downside protection and, through the buyout, a claim on the upside too.
  5. A fixed end date suits a life in motion. If a move, a new job or a change in household size is plausible within three years, a lease gives a scheduled exit instead of a sale at whatever the market happens to offer that month.
Opposition

The case for buying

  1. An electric car’s advantage compounds with time. Low energy and maintenance costs are the reason many people choose one. Those savings keep arriving for as long as you own the car, and the longest stretch of them comes after the loan is paid off. Leasing resets to a new payment every three years and never reaches the payment-free years.
  2. Cheap miles invite more miles. When driving costs a few cents a mile, people drive more. A lease’s mileage allowance turns that cheapness into an overage bill. An owner simply drives.
  3. The battery usually carries its own warranty. Many electric cars come with a battery warranty that runs well past a typical lease term; the exact terms are in the warranty booklet and differ by manufacturer. The owner benefits from it for its full length. A three-year lessee barely uses it.
  4. Weight wears things. Electric cars are heavy, and tires and suspension parts can wear accordingly. At a lease return, that wear is judged by someone else’s standard and billed. An owner deals with it on their own schedule and never pays an inspector’s price for a scuffed wheel.
  5. Owners can leave whenever they like. Selling a car you own in month twenty is a sale: you get what it’s worth and pay off what you owe. Leaving a lease in month twenty means early-termination arithmetic written by the other side.

Proposition replies

Point one mistakes whose savings they are. Electricity doesn’t care who holds the title. With illustrative inputs:

12,000 mi ÷ 100 × 30 kWh = 3,600 kWh a year
3,600 kWh × $0.15 = $540 a year, about 4.5¢ a mile

A lessee pays the same $540 as an owner. The only saving buying adds is the payment-free years, and those depend on the car still being reliable and wanted in year eight, which is precisely what nobody can yet promise. As for home charging equipment, it stays with the house and serves whatever you plug in next, leased or owned. The home charging calculator gives the same answer to both sides.

Opposition replies

The protection my opponent describes is real, but it isn’t free. The lessor prices the risk into the lease through the rent charge and the residual it chooses to set, and a cautious residual means a higher payment. If values hold up, the lessee has paid for depreciation that never happened, and the buyout “upside” means paying a second time, in taxes and fees, to keep a car they could have owned outright.

And the technology argument cuts both ways. A three-year cycle puts every lessee permanently in the steepest part of the depreciation curve, paying for the years when a car loses value fastest, over and over. The owner pays for them once.

Referee’s note

Both sides scored, and neither can win without facts only you have. Three decide it.

How long you keep cars. If your honest history is three or four years, most of the opposition’s case evaporates: you never reach the payment-free years or use the long battery warranty. If you drive cars into the ground, the proposition has to explain away a decade of low running costs.

How much you drive. Heavy mileage weakens leasing unless the allowance is bought up front, and even then it costs money.

How much uncertainty you’ll pay to avoid. Leasing is, in effect, buying protection against a bad resale outcome. Whether that protection is well priced is visible only by comparing the specific lease’s residual and rent charge with what similar cars are actually worth used, and that comparison changes model by model.

The motion is carried or defeated in your driveway, not here. The debate only tells you which three numbers to bring to it.
