Leasing

Twelve questions before a lease buyout

A lease buyout is a used-car purchase where you already know the car. That is a real advantage, and it is also the reason people skip questions they would ask any other seller.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Answer these in order if you can. The first four are about the price, the next four about the car, and the last four about you. A buyout that survives all twelve is one you can sign without second-guessing.

The price
  1. What is the purchase option price in my contract?

    It is set at signing, usually the residual value plus any purchase option fee, and it is the anchor for everything else. Find it in the lease itself before anyone quotes you a figure over the phone. Residual value, explained covers where that number came from.

  2. What is the actual payoff, today, in writing?

    Before the lease ends, the payoff is the purchase option price plus the payments you haven't made yet, give or take the leasing company's own calculation. Ask for a written quote with a good-through date. A verbal figure is a guess you can't hold anyone to.

    Ask, too, whether the quote changes depending on who completes the purchase. Some leasing companies handle a buyout directly with the lessee; others route it through a dealer, which can add a layer of fees. The contract and the leasing company will tell you which applies.

  3. What fees sit on top of that price?

    A purchase option fee, a documentation or processing fee if a dealer handles the transaction, and state title and registration charges can all appear. Some are set by the lease and can't be negotiated; a dealer's add-on processing fee sometimes can. Know which is which before the paperwork arrives.

  4. How will sales tax be handled?

    Treatment varies widely by state: what the tax is calculated on, whether any tax you paid during the lease counts, and who collects it. It can add a meaningful amount to the cost. Your state's revenue department or motor vehicle agency is the authority. Sales tax when you buy out a lease lists the questions to put to them.

The car
  1. What is the car worth on the open market?

    Compare the all-in buyout cost with what similar cars, same year, trim and mileage, are selling for. If the buyout is cheaper, you have equity. If it is more expensive, you'd be paying a premium for a car you know; that can still be rational, but decide it deliberately.

    Use prices from cars that actually sold or are realistically listed, and adjust for options and condition. The comparison only helps if you are honest about which of those cars your own resembles.

  2. What does it need in the next two years?

    Tires, brakes, a battery, a major scheduled service. As a lessee you may have deferred anything not strictly required. As an owner, those bills are yours. Add them to the price you're comparing.

  3. When does the factory warranty run out?

    Many leases end close to the point where basic coverage expires, by months or by miles. Check the warranty booklet for both limits. Owning a car just past its warranty is fine if you've budgeted for repairs; it is a shock if you haven't.

  4. Would an independent inspection find anything?

    You know how the car has been driven, but not necessarily what a mechanic would see on a lift. A pre-purchase inspection costs little next to the price of the car and may reveal a leak or a worn part you've grown used to.

    Familiarity cuts both ways. You know the car has never been in a serious accident on your watch, which a stranger's used car can't promise. But you also stop hearing the noise that started last winter. Let someone else listen.

You
  1. How long do I plan to keep it?

    A buyout makes most sense for someone who intends to drive the car for years, spreading the purchase across many post-warranty miles. If you might sell within a year, compare the buyout's cost with simply returning the car and buying something else.

  2. Would I pay cash or borrow?

    Cash avoids interest but empties savings; borrowing keeps a cushion but adds a finance charge and a monthly obligation. Whichever you're weighing, compare total cost, not payment. Paying cash for a lease buyout, or financing it sets out the trade-offs.

  3. What does the other path cost me?

    Returning the car may trigger a disposition fee and charges for excess miles or wear. Buying it avoids those. They are a legitimate part of the comparison, but they should tip a close decision, not justify an expensive one.

    Get the actual figures rather than estimates: your odometer reading against the allowance in the contract, the per-mile rate, and the wear standard. A pre-return inspection, where the leasing company offers one, turns a vague worry into a number.

  4. Does the car still fit my life?

    Three years is long enough for a commute, a household or a hobby to change. The best-priced buyout of the wrong car is still the wrong car. Ask the question plainly and let the numbers wait for the answer.

    Picture the next three years specifically: who rides in it, where it gets parked, how far it goes each week. If the answer is "the same as now," that is a strong point in the buyout's favor that no spreadsheet will capture.

If you get to the end and the answers are mostly good, the decision is probably already made, and you can spend your energy on the logistics: getting the payoff quote refreshed, arranging payment, and confirming how and when the title will be transferred into your name. If several answers are uncomfortable, that is information too. Returning a leased car is not a failure; it is the option the lease was designed around.

Write your answers down. The last month of a lease tends to be busy, and a list you completed calmly three months out is worth more than the same questions asked in a hurry at the dealer's desk.
