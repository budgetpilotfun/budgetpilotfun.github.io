Buying & Selling

Money mistakes people make when buying a car

Notes, not a lecture. Each one stands alone; read them in any order, skip the ones that don't apply, and keep the one that stings.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read
No. 01 · the question at the desk

Answering "what payment are you looking for?"

It is asked in a friendly tone, and the honest answer hands over the one number that lets a deal be built backward. Price, term, rate and trade-in can all be adjusted to hit a payment, and a payment you are happy with says nothing about whether the car cost too much. A calmer reply: "I'm looking at the total price first. We can talk about payments after."

No. 02 · the long term

Letting the term grow until the payment fits.

An illustrative $25,000 financed at 9% APR, computed two ways:

48 months: $622.13/mo · interest $4,862.05
72 months: $450.64/mo · interest $7,445.97
Lower payment by $171.49 · more interest by $2,583.92

The rate is a round number for arithmetic, not a quote — a longer term can carry a higher rate as well, which would widen the gap. The bigger cost is less visible: two extra years during which the car is aging faster than the balance is falling.

No. 03 · the missing total

Negotiating without an out-the-door number.

A discount off the advertised price means little if fees and add-ons reappear lower on the page. Ask for the total — price, taxes, registration and title charges, every dealer fee — in writing, before discussing anything else. Asking for the out-the-door price walks through what should be in it.

No. 04 · four numbers, one blur

Letting price, trade-in, financing and add-ons merge.

When all four are discussed at once, a generous trade-in figure can be paid for by a firmer price, or a price cut can quietly return as a longer term. Settle them one at a time, in writing, in any order you like — just not together.

No. 05 · the call not made

Buying before getting an insurance quote.

Two cars at the same price can carry very different premiums, depending on the model, its repair costs and your own record. Calling your insurer with the VIN of the car you're considering takes minutes. Discovering the premium after signing takes the rest of the loan.

No. 06 · the empty cushion

Putting the emergency fund into the down payment.

A bigger down payment reduces what you borrow, which is good. Emptying savings to make it leaves the household one flat tire and a vet bill away from a credit card balance at a far higher rate. A down payment should come from money you can do without, not money you're counting on.

No. 07 · hour three

Deciding on add-ons while exhausted.

Service contracts, coatings, etching, tire-and-wheel plans and similar products are usually offered at the very end, when you have been sitting a long time and want to leave. Financed, each one also collects interest. Ask for the list and the prices, take it home, and decide on a different day. The add-ons that end up inside your loan shows what they cost once financed.

No. 08 · the skipped inspection

Buying a used car without an independent inspection.

A mechanic who works for you, not the seller, looking at the car on a lift before money changes hands. The fee is small next to what it can find, and a seller who won't allow one has told you something important without saying a word.

No. 09 · the recall list

Not checking open recalls.

Recall repairs are generally done at no charge, but only if someone knows about them. Enter the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls before buying, and ask the seller about anything open.

No. 10 · "we'll fix it after"

Signing a contract that doesn't match what was agreed.

Read the numbers in the retail installment contract — amount financed, APR, term, payment, total of payments — against what you were told. If something is wrong, it gets corrected before you sign, not afterward. Blank spaces get filled or crossed out.

No. 11 · driving home early

Assuming financing is final because you have the keys.

Some dealer-arranged deals are delivered before the financing is actually finalized, and the terms can come back different. Ask plainly whether the financing is final, get the answer in writing, and hold on to your trade-in's paperwork until it is. The rules around this vary by state; consumerfinance.gov has plain-language guidance on auto loans.

No. 12 · today only

Buying because an offer expires.

Deadlines exist to shorten your thinking. A real price on a real car rarely disappears overnight, and if it does, another car will come along. The decision that has to be made in the next hour is usually the one worth sleeping on.

No. 13 · the first year

Budgeting only for the payment.

Insurance, registration, fuel, tires and maintenance all start on day one, and some arrive in lumps. A car that fits the budget at the payment line can fail it by the first renewal notice.

No. 14 · the old loan

Assuming a trade-in's loan simply disappears.

When you trade in a car you still owe on, the dealer generally pays off that loan — and if you owe more than the car is worth, the difference has to go somewhere, very often into the new loan. Ask to see the payoff amount and where the shortfall, if any, appears on the paperwork. Then keep checking your old lender's statements until it shows a zero balance.

No. 15 · the loan shopped last

Choosing the car first and the financing second.

By the time the car is picked, the test drive done and the price agreed, energy for comparing loans is gone, and whatever is offered at the desk tends to be accepted. Looking at financing options before shopping gives you a point of comparison. What a preapproval actually is explains what that step does and doesn't lock in.
