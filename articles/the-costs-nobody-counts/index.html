Running Costs

Parking, tolls and the costs nobody counts

A composite household wrote down every small car charge for a week. Nineteen of them, none large, and an annual total nobody had ever seen.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Monday started with a toll.

That is how the diary below opens — a record of one illustrative week in which every car cost that wasn't fuel, insurance or a loan payment got written down on the day it happened. The household is a composite, not a real family, and every amount is a round placeholder rather than a typical price. What's real is the pattern: small charges that never reach a budget because none of them feels big enough to count.

Each entry is tagged by category. The tally and the annual figure come at the end.

Nineteen separate charges in seven days, none larger than $35, adding up to $149. Nothing on the list would make anyone rethink a car, and that is the point: these costs escape budgets not because they're hidden but because each one is too small to register.

Why each category slips through

Tolls are the most invisible of the four because they're automated. A transponder account tops itself up from a card when the balance runs low, so what reaches your bank statement is an occasional round-number refill, not eight crossings. The charges happened on Monday through Friday; the evidence arrives weeks later, lumped together. Some toll systems also price differently depending on how you pay or the time of day, so the per-crossing figure on your account statement is worth reading at least once.

Parking hides by scattering. Meters, apps, garages and event lots each go through a different channel, and apps may add a convenience charge on top of the posted rate. Friday's garage in the diary is the classic case: charged alongside dinner, it will be remembered as the cost of going out rather than the cost of driving there.

Fines hide by being treated as bad luck. One ticket feels like an accident, but a routine that depends on a meter outlasting a meeting will produce tickets on some schedule. A phone reminder or a longer-duration spot is often cheaper than the occasional fine.

Upkeep — washes, fluid, air — hides by being tiny. Individually these are the least important line. They are on the list mainly because a budget that forgets them will be short by exactly that much, every month.

Annualizing without fooling yourself

The tempting move is to multiply the week by 52. That gives $7,748 a year, and it's wrong, because not every week looks like this one. The ticket was unusual. The game was unusual. The car wash is monthly. Multiplying a single week treats the week's accidents as a lifestyle.

The better method sorts each category by how often it really recurs, then annualizes at that rate. The frequencies here are assumptions made for this composite household:

$3,711 a year is about $309 a month. That's less than half the lazy estimate, and still a figure most households have never seen written down, because it's spread across a transponder account, a parking app, a debit card and a restaurant receipt. For comparison, it can rival a car payment or an insurance premium — lines that do get budgeted, because they arrive as single bills.

It also changes the arithmetic of other decisions. If a job goes remote, the $2,208 in weekday tolls and parking disappears along with the commute; when you stop commuting follows what else shifts over the first year. And any honest monthly total, like the one built in what a car really costs per month, needs a line for this category or it will run short every month without anyone knowing why.

Running your own week

Keep a note on your phone for seven days and log every car-related charge that isn't fuel, insurance or a loan payment: tolls, meters, garages, apps, washes, fluids, air, fines, even the vending-machine ice scraper. At the end, tally by category, decide honestly how often each recurs, and annualize the way the table does. Check the transponder statement and the parking app's history as well; they'll catch what you forgot to write down.

One week of noticing, one table, and a number goes into the budget that was always being spent anyway. For this household, the number is $309 a month.
