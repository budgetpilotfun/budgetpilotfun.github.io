Buying & Selling

Making the first offer

What follows never happened. It is a composite, assembled to show the moves that turn up in ordinary car negotiations, with a note in the margin beside each line.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

The negotiation below is invented. The people are placeholders, the dealership is nowhere, and the prices are round numbers chosen to make the arithmetic easy to follow. What is real is the sequence: nearly every line is a move that turns up again and again when people buy cars, and the notes in the margin say what each one is doing.

Setting. A used-car lot. A five-year-old sedan listed at $18,900. The buyer has done homework at home: an all-in target of $19,600, a walk-away point of $20,000, and a rough estimate that tax, the doc fee and state charges will add about $1,700 at this price, putting the listed car near $20,600 out the door. Financing is arranged elsewhere. Salesperson S, buyer B.

S"So, what were you hoping to keep the monthly payment around?"

01The payment question. Friendly, and it moves the talk onto the one number that can be stretched with a longer term. Answering it first gives away more than it seems.

B"I'd like to leave payments aside for now and talk about the out-the-door price."

02The redirect. No argument, just a different frame. The total includes every fee, so nothing can be added back quietly later.

S"Sure. It's listed at $18,900, which is honestly already below market for this one."

03The anchor, with a claim attached. It may even be true. It is also unverified, and it sets $18,900 as the point everything moves down from.

B"I've looked at similar ones within an hour's drive, same year, similar miles. They're listed from about $17,800 to $19,400. This one sits in the middle."

04Evidence, calmly stated. Not a rebuttal, a counter-anchor. Note "listed": asking prices, not selling prices. The buyer does not pretend otherwise.

B"I'm prepared to do $19,200 out the door today. Tax, fees, everything."

05The first offer. Below the $19,600 target, so there is room to move, but not so low it ends the conversation. It is a specific number, which reads as calculated, and it is stated once, without apology.

S"Out the door? The doc fee alone is $399, and tax is tax. That puts the car itself around $17,500. I really don't think my manager goes there."

06The salesperson does the arithmetic. Useful: the argument is now about a specific gap, not about whether the buyer is serious.

B"I understand. It's my number based on what I've seen. Would you ask?"

07Holding still. The pull here is to explain, soften, or offer more before hearing anything back. That is negotiating against yourself. The buyer does none of it.

S(returns after some minutes) "He can do $20,100 out the door, and he'll add all-weather floor mats."

08A counter plus a sweetener. The price moved about $500 from the buyer's estimate of the listed total. Whatever the mats are worth, they are not money off, and turning them down costs the buyer nothing.

B"Thanks. I don't need the mats. I can come up to $19,500."

09A smaller step. $300 up, after the dealer came down about $500. Shrinking steps signal that the floor is close without saying where it is.

S"What if we met at $19,800 and you financed through us? I think we could get the payment to a place you'd like."

10The bundle. Price, financing and payment back in one sentence. A lower price tied to dealer financing can be recovered in the loan terms, so the two are best judged separately.

B"I've got financing handled, thanks. If $19,650 out the door works, I can sign today. If it doesn't, I understand, and I'll leave you my number."

11A final offer with a real edge. It unbundles the financing, names a price inside the buyer's limits, and makes leaving an ordinary outcome instead of a threat. The walk-away point itself is never mentioned.

S(after another visit to the manager) "Okay. $19,650. Let me print the buyer's order."

12Not the end. The agreement is only as good as the paperwork. Every line on the buyer's order should add up to $19,650, with nothing new on it.

The dealer's opening figure is the buyer's own estimate of the listed price out the door, so it carries a tilde. Against it, the finished deal is roughly $950 lower. Against the buyer's own card, it landed $50 above the target and $350 under the walk-away point, which is what a successful negotiation usually looks like from the inside: not a triumph, just a number you chose ahead of time, more or less met.

What the transcript leaves out

Most of the work happened before line 01. The buyer arrived knowing three numbers, set the way we describe in three numbers to set before you shop, and knowing that the out-the-door figure was the one to argue about, for the reasons in asking for the out-the-door price. With those in hand, nearly every line above becomes easy to say, because none of it requires deciding anything on the spot.

Real conversations are also less tidy. There are longer waits, a second salesperson, a manager who appears in person, a trade-in that muddies everything, or a flat "that's our price." Sometimes the dealer's floor sits above your walk-away point, and the correct ending to the transcript is the buyer standing up at line 11 and leaving. That is not a failed negotiation. It is the card working.

And line 12 matters more than it looks. Before signing, read the buyer's order line by line; our walk through every line on a buyer's order covers what should be there and what should not. A price agreed in conversation is a starting point for the paperwork, not a guarantee of it.
