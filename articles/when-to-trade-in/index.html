Buying & Selling

When to trade in

There is no single right age to trade in a car. There are, though, a handful of moments in its life when the money changes character, and they are worth knowing before you choose.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

There is no correct year to trade in a car, whatever a service advisor or an advertisement suggests. What exists instead is a sequence. Every car that is bought, financed and kept passes through roughly the same money moments in roughly the same order, and each one tilts the trade-in question one way or the other. The timeline below lays them out for an illustrative car bought new with a 60-month loan. The year markings are for orientation only; your car, loan and driving will put the moments in different places.

Illustrative. Real timing depends on the car, the loan, the down payment and the miles.

The moments, one by one

1

Drive away

The first owner of a new car absorbs the steepest part of its depreciation, and much of that happens in the early years. You do not see it as a bill, which is what makes it easy to forget. We treat it as a real cost in depreciation, the bill that never arrives.

Trade-in here: the most expensive time to do it. You pay for the fastest-falling years and keep none of the slower ones.

2

Equity crossover

Early in a loan, the balance can sit above what the car would fetch, especially with a small down payment or a long term. The red band is that stretch. At some point, as payments reduce the balance faster than the car loses value, the lines cross. From here on the car is worth more than you owe.

Trade-in here: the earliest point at which trading does not carry old debt into a new loan. Before it, the shortfall has to be paid or rolled forward, which is covered in rolling negative equity into the next loan.

3

Factory warranty ends

Coverage length differs by manufacturer and by component, and is usually limited by both time and miles, so check your own warranty booklet. When it ends, the cost of an unexpected failure moves from the manufacturer to you.

Trade-in here: reasonable if you value predictability above all, but notice that a car does not become unreliable on the day its warranty expires.

4

Last payment

On a 60-month loan, the payment stops in year five. The car is still worth something, still depreciating, but now slowly, and it costs only what it takes to run and insure it.

Trade-in here: the moment many people do trade, and often the moment it makes least sense. The green band is where a car tends to be cheapest to own.

5

Bigger scheduled items

Somewhere past the early years, tires, brakes, a battery and the larger services listed in the owner's manual tend to cluster. They are expected, not signs of decline. A few hundred or a couple of thousand dollars here is usually far less than a year of payments on a replacement.

Trade-in here: tempting, because the bills are visible. Compare the bill to a year of payments before deciding.

6

Repairs approach a payment

Eventually a year's repair bills, not counting routine maintenance, may start to look like what a year of payments on a newer car would cost. That comparison is the one to watch. Unreliability has costs beyond the invoice as well: missed work, towing, rental cars.

Trade-in here: the case becomes genuine, especially if the car has become unpredictable.

7

Repair exceeds value

A single repair estimate larger than the car is worth is the natural end of the line for most owners. Safety counts here too: a car whose structure or brakes are in doubt is not worth defending with money.

Trade-in here: expect little for it as a trade. A private sale or selling it as-is may do better.

Placing your own car on the line

The illustrative years are a sketch. To find where your car actually sits, four pieces of paper do most of the work.

The payoff figure. Your lender can give you the amount needed to close the loan today, which is not always the same as the balance on your statement, because interest accrues daily. Compare it with what the car would realistically sell or trade for, and you know whether you are in the red band or past moment 2.

The warranty booklet. It states what is covered, for how long and for how many miles. Components often have different limits, so a car can be out of one coverage and inside another.

The maintenance schedule. The owner's manual lists what is due and roughly when. Looking ahead a year or two tells you whether moment 5 is approaching and what it is likely to involve.

The last twelve months of repair receipts. Add up the unscheduled ones. Set the total beside twelve payments on the car you would replace it with. That single comparison is moment 6, and it is more honest than any rule about age or mileage.

Reading the timeline

Laid end to end, the moments suggest a plain rule of thumb: the cheapest stretch of ownership is usually the green band, after the loan and before the repairs, and each trade-in resets you to the top of the red one. That does not make keeping a car forever correct. Needs change: a family grows, a commute doubles, a car stops being safe for a new driver. But when the reason to trade is simply that the payments have ended and something newer looks nice, the timeline is worth a second look.

Two practical notes. Trade-in offers vary between dealers and often come in below a private sale; getting more than one figure helps, and getting paid safely when you sell privately covers the alternative. And if you are still inside the red band when you want out, the honest options are to pay the gap, wait, or accept a bigger next loan, in roughly that order of preference.
