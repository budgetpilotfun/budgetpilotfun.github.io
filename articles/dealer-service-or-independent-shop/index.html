Running Costs

Dealer service or independent shop

Nine criteria, two kinds of shop, scores out of five. Added up evenly it's nearly a tie, which is why the weighting is where the decision lives.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Neither wins outright, and the scorecard below shows why. A franchised dealer's service department and a good independent shop are strong in almost opposite places, so the better choice depends less on which is "better" than on which criteria your car, at its current age, actually needs.

The scores are our judgment, on a one-to-five scale, of how each type of shop typically performs. They are not measurements and not survey results — there is wide variation within each type, and a particular dealer or independent can beat or miss every score here. Treat the card as a structured way to think, then score the two or three real shops near you.

The totals are nearly tied, and that is the first lesson: added up evenly, the two kinds of shop come out about the same. What separates them is weighting. First, the reasoning behind each row.

Why each row scored the way it did

  1. 1

    Hourly labor rate

    Dealer service departments often post higher hourly rates than independents, though not always. The rate is only half the bill; the other half is how many hours a job is billed at. Ask both shops for the rate and the hours quoted, and compare totals rather than rates.

    Dealer 2 · Independent 4
  2. 2

    Warranty and recall work

    Repairs covered by the manufacturer's warranty, and recall repairs, are generally handled through the manufacturer's dealer network — an independent can't usually do them on the manufacturer's dime. This is the dealer's clearest advantage. Check your warranty booklet for how covered repairs are arranged, and look up open recalls by VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.

    Dealer 5 · Independent 1
  3. 3

    Model-specific diagnostics and software

    Dealers have the manufacturer's own diagnostic equipment, service bulletins and software updates. Many independents invest in comparable tools, especially shops that specialize in one or two makes, which is why they get a three rather than a one. For a new model with an electrical oddity, the dealer's access often matters.

    Dealer 5 · Independent 3
  4. 4

    Choice of parts

    Dealers generally fit the manufacturer's parts. Independents can usually fit those too, or offer aftermarket parts at different price points. More choice is useful — though a cheaper part is only a saving if it is of adequate quality, which is a fair question to ask.

    Dealer 3 · Independent 4
  5. 5

    Knowing who works on your car

    At a small shop you can often talk to the person who will do the work. At a larger service department you usually deal with a service advisor who relays between you and the technician. Direct conversation tends to make diagnosis faster and makes it easier to judge a recommendation.

    Dealer 2 · Independent 4
  6. 6

    Convenience extras

    Loaner cars, shuttles, waiting rooms, online booking and longer hours are more common at dealers. Some independents offer them. If a day without the car costs you real money, this row carries more weight than it looks.

    Dealer 4 · Independent 2
  7. 7

    Older, out-of-warranty cars

    Once a car is well past warranty, the dealer's main advantages fade while its rates don't. Independents often see a lot of older cars and may be more willing to suggest a repair rather than a replacement of a whole assembly.

    Dealer 2 · Independent 4
  8. 8

    A paper trail for resale

    Both produce itemized invoices with dates and mileage. What matters to a future buyer is that the records exist and are complete, so keep every invoice, wherever the work was done.

    Dealer 4 · Independent 4
  9. 9

    Pressure to add services

    Upselling exists at both, driven by individual shops rather than the category. The defense is the same everywhere: know your maintenance schedule. The service menu, translated covers the usual suspects.

    Dealer 3 · Independent 3

One worry to set aside

A common belief is that using an independent shop for routine maintenance voids a new-car warranty. The Federal Trade Commission's consumer guidance says otherwise: you don't have to use the dealer for maintenance or repairs to keep a warranty in effect, and it calls denying coverage for that reason illegal — see the FTC's page on auto warranties and service contracts. The same page notes the exception: where the warranty does the work for free, the manufacturer can require its own facilities. What you must do is follow the maintenance schedule and keep the receipts. Your warranty booklet sets out the specific conditions for your car.

Weighting the card for your car

The fair way to use a scorecard is to double the rows that matter most to you and recount. Two illustrative cases show how far that moves the result.

A two-year-old car, still under warranty

Warranty work (row 2) and model-specific diagnostics (row 3) matter most. Doubling those two rows adds 10 to the dealer and 4 to the independent.

Dealer 40Independent 33

A twelve-year-old car, paid off

Labor rate (row 1), knowing the mechanic (row 5) and older-car experience (row 7) matter most. Doubling those adds 6 to the dealer and 12 to the independent.

Dealer 36Independent 41

The ranking flips as the car ages, which matches how many owners behave in practice: dealer service while the warranty runs, an independent afterward. Nothing requires that split, and a trusted dealer service department can be worth staying with for years. But the card explains why the switch is common, and why it tends to happen right around when the warranty ends.

Two other situations change the weights. If you are considering a service contract, check where it lets you have covered work done before signing; some limit your choice of shop, and that shifts rows 2 and 7. Extended warranty arithmetic covers whether such a contract is worth its price. And if the car will be leased and returned, the lessor's view of service records matters — the lease agreement is the place to find it.

To score your own shortlist, ask each shop the same five things: the hourly rate, the book hours for one job you know is coming, whether they'll show you worn parts, which parts they fit, and what happens if the car isn't ready on time. The answers will fill in most of the rows above — and they will tell you more about that particular shop than any category ever could.
