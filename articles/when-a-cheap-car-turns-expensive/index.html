Running Costs

When a cheap car turns out expensive

The cheaper car wins on the first day by eight thousand dollars. This is a race chart of the next five years, and of the three assumptions that decide who finishes ahead.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Two illustrative cars, driven the same 12,000 miles a year for five years by the same composite owner. Nothing here describes a real model; the numbers are round on purpose so the arithmetic is easy to follow and easy to swap for your own.

Car A, the bargain

Bought for $6,000 cash. Older, higher mileage, 22 mpg. Repairs assumed to climb each year: $800, $1,400, $2,200, $2,600, $3,000.

Car B, the stretch

Bought for $14,000 cash. Younger, 30 mpg. Repairs assumed to stay modest: $300, $400, $600, $800, $1,000.

Fuel is priced at an illustrative $3.50 a gallon. Car A burns 12,000 ÷ 22 = about 545 gallons a year, roughly $1,909. Car B burns 12,000 ÷ 30 = 400 gallons, or $1,400. The chart below adds purchase price, repairs and fuel cumulatively, year by year. Insurance, registration and tires are left out to keep the chart readable. They would lift both lines, and not necessarily by the same amount: an older car can be cheaper to insure, particularly if you no longer carry collision on it, which would narrow the gap further in the bargain's favor.

Illustrative only. Cumulative purchase + repairs + fuel; resale value not yet subtracted.

Reading the race

At the start line Car A is $8,000 ahead, and for four years it stays ahead. After year one the gap is $6,991; after year two, $5,482; after year three, $3,373; after year four, $1,064. Each lap, the bargain gives back more ground, because its repair bill grows while Car B's barely moves and it burns about $509 more fuel every year.

Somewhere a little under halfway through year five, the lines cross. By the end of that year Car A has cost $25,545 and Car B $24,100, a difference of $1,445 in the stretch car's favor.

That crossing is modest, and it arrives late. An owner who sold Car A after three years would have spent $16,127 against Car B's $19,500 and could reasonably feel the bargain had paid off, before counting what either car was then worth. How long you keep the car is part of the answer, because the steep end of A's repair curve only matters to the person still holding it.

The bigger swing comes when the race ends and both cars are sold.

With resale counted, Car B comes out $7,945 ahead. But notice how much of that verdict rests on a single cell: the assumption that a $14,000 car is still worth $8,000 five years later while the $6,000 car is worth $1,500. That is an illustration, not a depreciation forecast. Real resale depends on the model, the mileage, the condition and the market the year you sell.

The three assumptions that pick the winner

The shape of Car A's repair curve. We gave the bargain a steep, rising curve. Flatten it to $800 every year and Car A spends $6,000 + $4,000 + $9,545 = $19,545 over five years, well under Car B's $24,100. The lines never cross. Net of the same resale values, A comes to $18,045 against B's $16,100, so B still wins, but by $1,945 rather than $7,945. A cheap car with a clean mechanical history and a known owner can run that flat. One with warning signs you chose to ignore probably will not. Budgeting maintenance as a car ages covers how repair costs tend to cluster over a car's life.

What Car B is worth at the finish. Knock B's resale down to $5,000 and, with A's flatter repair curve, the result flips: B nets $19,100 and A $18,045, so the bargain wins by $1,055. The stretch car's advantage lives largely in what it still is at the end, which is why a newer car that depreciates hard can lose this race.

How you paid. Both cars here were bought with cash. If Car B had needed a loan and Car A had not, B's line would carry interest on top, and it would climb more steeply from the start. Borrowing to reach the "better" car can erase the advantage the chart shows.

What the chart leaves out

Money is only one kind of cost. An unreliable car also costs mornings: tows, missed shifts, rides borrowed, rental days while a part is on order. Those don't appear on a line chart, but they are real, and for someone whose job depends on arriving on time they can matter more than a thousand dollars either way. The reverse holds too: a cheap car that has already depreciated is one you can drive without fretting over every door ding.

The takeaway is not "never buy cheap." It is that a purchase price is only the first dot on a line, and the slope after it is what you are really buying. Before choosing, sketch your own two lines with honest guesses for repairs, fuel and resale, or work it per mile as in your real cost per mile. If the cheap car only wins when every assumption goes its way, it was never cheap.
