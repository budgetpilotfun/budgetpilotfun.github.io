Leasing

Lease or buy, six years out

Comparing a lease and a loan by their monthly payments settles nothing. Comparing them over six years, with what each leaves behind, settles quite a lot, at least for one set of assumptions.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Two households need a car for the next six years. One leases, then leases again when the first term ends. The other buys the same kind of car with a five-year loan and keeps it. Every figure below is illustrative, chosen to be plausible and easy to follow, not to represent any market, lender or lessor. Taxes are treated as already folded into the prices; insurance, fuel and routine servicing are left out on the assumption that they would be similar for both.

The lease household
  • Lease 1: $2,000 at signing, $400/month, 36 months
  • Lease 2: $2,000 at signing, $420/month, 36 months
  • $400 turn-in fee at the end of each lease
  • Stays within miles; no damage charges
The buying household
  • $32,000 all-in, $4,000 down
  • $28,000 at 6% APR for 60 months = $541.32/month
  • Out-of-warranty repairs, years 4–6: $1,400, $900, $1,200
  • Car worth about $14,000 at the end of year 6

Yearly figures are rounded to the dollar from exact payments; the running totals are built from the unrounded numbers, which is why a column may not add to the last dollar by eye.

What the columns show

Read down the left and the lease is cheaper in cash every single year until the last one. It is cheaper in cumulative cash at every point too. After six years the lease household has paid out $5,659 less than the buyers.

Then the tally turns it over. The buyers have a car, and if it really is worth around $14,000, their net cost falls to $25,979. The lease household's net cost is simply what it paid. Under these assumptions buying comes out $8,341 ahead over six years.

Notice where that advantage comes from. It is not the monthly payment, which was higher for the buyers in five years out of six. It is year six, when the loan is gone and the car is still there, plus the value sitting in the driveway at the end. The purchase wins by outlasting its own loan. Stop the comparison at year three and the buyers have spent $6,687 more with a car that is worth something but has not yet had time to justify itself.

Where the assumptions could flip it

The resale value. The $14,000 is the single largest number in the buyers' favor and the least certain one in the model. At $10,000, their net rises to $29,979 and the gap shrinks to $4,341. What a six-year-old car fetches depends on the model, the miles and the market that year. The mechanism is covered in depreciation, the bill that never arrives.

The repairs. We gave the buyers $3,500 of repairs across years four to six. A single major failure could double that. If year six had brought a $4,200 repair instead of $1,200, the buyers' net would be $28,979, still $5,341 ahead, but with a much worse year to live through. The lease household, driving a car under three years old for the whole period, largely sidesteps that variance.

The lease side's own risks. This model assumes no excess miles and no wear charges at either turn-in. A household that drives more than its allowance, or returns a car with curb-rashed wheels and a cracked windshield, adds real money to the left column at years three and six.

The lease prices themselves. We raised the second lease's payment by $20 a month, a guess and nothing more. Lease payments move with the car's price, its residual and the money factor, all of which can shift in three years. If the second lease had cost $480 a month instead of $420, the left column would carry another $2,160 over years four to six, and the buyers' lead would grow to $10,501. The pieces that drive that number are taken apart in how a lease payment is calculated.

The down payment's alternative use. The buyers put $4,000 down against the lessees' $2,000. The extra $2,000 could have earned something elsewhere. That opportunity cost is real but, at most savings yields, small next to the gaps above.

What happens in year seven. This is the quiet argument for buying. The lease household enters year seven with a new payment and a new signing cost. The buyers enter it with repairs and nothing else. Extend the comparison and, absent a catastrophic repair, the right-hand column keeps pulling away.

Running your own

The method transfers even if the numbers do not. Get real quotes for both sides: a lease with its due-at-signing, payment and end-of-lease fees, and a purchase with its price, down payment and loan terms. Decide how long you will actually keep a car, not how long you intend to. Fill in the same two columns year by year, with a realistic repair allowance for the owned car after warranty, and then put an honest resale estimate at the bottom of the right-hand column. The side that wins is the answer for that set of numbers, and nothing more general than that.
