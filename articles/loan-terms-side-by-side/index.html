Borrowing

36, 48, 60, 72, 84 months

Same car, same rate, same amount borrowed. Only the calendar changes. Read the numbers top to bottom, then read the footers left to right, because that is where the case is made.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Illustrative loan: $28,000 financed at 7.5% APR in every column, no down payment shown, simple interest, level monthly payments. Totals use the rounded payment; on a real contract the final payment adjusts by a few cents. We held the rate flat on purpose so that only the term moves. In practice a lender may price longer terms differently, and if a longer term comes with a higher rate, the right-hand columns look worse than they do here.

The worst exchange rate. $54.65 less per month, for $1,218.84 more interest.

Seven years is long enough for the loan to outlast tires, a battery and possibly bigger repairs. You would be paying for the car and fixing it in the same years. At this length the useful question is no longer about the payment. It is about the car.

The pattern in the footers

Read the five footers in order and one thing repeats. Each added year buys less monthly relief than the year before it, and each costs a little more interest than the year before it. $193.96, then $115.95, then $76.94, then $54.65 of relief. $1,141.56, then $1,167.12, then $1,193.04, then $1,218.84 of cost. The two lines move in opposite directions the whole way across. There is no point on the page where stretching gets cheaper.

Put the ends side by side and the full spread is stark. The 84-month column roughly halves the payment of the 36-month one, $429.47 against $870.97, and charges $4,720.56 more interest for the privilege. That is not a verdict against long loans in every case. Someone who needs a reliable car to keep a job, and can only carry the lower payment, may be making a sensible trade. It is a verdict against choosing the term last, after the car, as the knob that makes an expensive car fit.

What the middle rows are for

The "owed at month 24" and "owed at month 36" figures are the rows people skip, and they carry the second argument. On the 36-month loan you owe $10,039.30 after two years. On the 84-month loan you owe $21,432.96 at the same point, having made the same number of payments. Life does not check the loan schedule before it changes. A move, a new baby, a job in another city: if you need to sell or swap the car early, the balance in that row is what you have to clear, and anything the car won't cover comes out of your pocket or into the next loan. How an auto loan payment is built shows why the balance drops so slowly early on, and the longer the term, the longer that slow stretch lasts.

Using the grid on a real quote

Ask for the same deal quoted at two terms, in writing, before choosing. Then look past the monthly figure to the "total of payments" line on each contract, which is where the whole column's cost sits; reading a retail installment contract shows where to find it. Subtract one total from the other to get the extra interest, subtract one monthly payment from the other to get the relief, and set the two numbers side by side. That pair is your own footer. If the arithmetic feels unkind, that is the grid doing its job.

A last check, the one that settles most of these decisions: take the shortest term whose payment you could still make in a bad month, not a good one. If none of the five fit, the term was never the problem. The price of the car was, and the car you can afford vs the car you're approved for is the place to start.
