Leasing

How a lease payment is calculated

Three terms, one sum. Once you have seen where each piece comes from, a lease quote stops being a number you are handed and becomes a number you can check.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Most people meet a lease payment as a finished figure on a worksheet. It can be taken apart, and because it is built from only a handful of inputs, it can also be put back together by hand. What follows is that reassembly, done the way a textbook would do it: name the quantities, write the relationships, then substitute numbers.

Nothing here is any leasing company's formula stated as law. It is the standard structure most closed-end consumer leases follow, and your contract's disclosure section will show you which figures your lessor actually used.

Given

C
adjusted capitalized cost: the price of the car for leasing purposes, after any cap cost reduction, plus any fees rolled in
R
residual value: what the contract says the car will be worth at the end
n
term, in months
MF
money factor: the lease's finance rate, written as a small decimal
t
tax rate applied to the monthly payment, where your state taxes it that way

Part one: paying for what you use up

(1)value consumed = C − R

You take the car at C and hand back something the contract has already priced at R. The gap between them is the part of the car you used up. That is the core of the deal.

(2)D = (C − R) ÷ n

Spread evenly across the term, the value consumed becomes the monthly depreciation fee, D. Every month carries an equal share, regardless of how fast the car's market value is actually falling.

Part two: paying for the money

The lessor has its money tied up in the car the whole time. Your balance starts at C and ends at R, so on average the lessor is carrying roughly halfway between the two.

(3)average balance ≈ (C + R) ÷ 2

Not a sum to pay; just the midpoint of a balance falling from C to R.

(4)monthly interest ≈ (C + R) ÷ 2 × APR ÷ 1200

An annual percentage rate divided by 12 gives a monthly rate; dividing by another 100 turns a percentage into a decimal. Hence 1200.

(5)= (C + R) × APR ÷ 2400

Fold the “÷ 2” from line (3) into the 1200 and you get 2400.

(6)MF = APR ÷ 2400

This is the whole secret of the money factor. It is the rate with the averaging and the monthly conversion already baked in, which is why it looks so oddly small.

(7)F = (C + R) × MF

Substituting (6) into (5) gives the monthly rent charge, F. Note the plus sign. People who expect to see C − R here are surprised, but the rent charge is levied on money outstanding, and the residual is money outstanding right up to the last day.

Part three: assembling the payment

(8)base payment P₀ = D + F
(9)P = P₀ × (1 + t)

This line is the one that varies most. Some states tax each monthly payment, some tax the full price or the total of payments up front, and some add local taxes on top. Line (9) models the monthly method only. Your state’s motor vehicle or revenue agency, and the tax line on your lease contract, tell you which applies.

Worked example

The inputs below are illustrative, chosen to keep the arithmetic clean. They are not a quote, a typical deal, or anyone's current rate.

Let

C
$30,000
R
$18,000
n
36 months
MF
0.00125
t
6% (illustrative only)
(2′)D = (30,000 − 18,000) ÷ 36 = 12,000 ÷ 36 = $333.33
(7′)F = (30,000 + 18,000) × 0.00125 = 48,000 × 0.00125 = $60.00
(8′)P₀ = 333.33 + 60.00 = $393.33
(9′)P = 393.33 × 1.06 = 393.33 + 23.60 = $416.93
∴ P = $416.93 per month
of which $333.33 buys the car’s decline,
$60.00 rents the money,
$23.60 is tax.

As a side result, line (6) run backwards tells you what that money factor means as an annual rate: 0.00125 × 2400 = 3.00%. That conversion gets a page of its own in the money factor, and how to convert it.

Corollaries

Lowering C is worth more than it looks. A dollar off the capitalized cost appears twice: once in (C − R), where it cuts the depreciation fee by 1/36 of a dollar a month, and once in (C + R), where it trims the rent charge too. In the example, $1,000 off C saves $1,000 ÷ 36 = $27.78 in depreciation plus $1,000 × 0.00125 = $1.25 in rent, or $29.03 a month before tax.

A higher residual cuts one term and raises the other. Raising R by $1,000 lowers D by $27.78 but raises F by $1.25, a net drop of $26.53. That is why leases on cars expected to hold their value tend to price well, and why the residual deserves its own look in residual value, explained.

Money down is not a discount. A cap cost reduction lowers C, and so the payment, but it is the same money paid earlier. If the car is totaled early, that cash is generally gone. Whether gap coverage or your contract offsets any of that is something to read for, not assume.

The term changes only D directly. Stretching n spreads the same C − R over more months, but in practice a longer lease usually comes with a lower residual, so both inputs move at once. Recompute rather than guess.

Fees hide in C. An acquisition fee, add-on products, or a rolled-in balance all inflate the capitalized cost and flow through both lines. The disclosure section of a consumer lease itemizes what went into the gross capitalized cost; that list is where to look first.

Checking a real quote

Ask for five figures: the adjusted capitalized cost, the residual, the term, the money factor, and how tax is applied. With those, lines (2), (7), (8) and (9) take a phone calculator and a minute. If your result and the quoted payment disagree by more than a few cents of rounding, something is in the deal that has not been named yet, and the difference, multiplied back out, often tells you roughly how big it is. Divide the gap by (1 + t), then by (1/n + MF), and you have an estimate of how much extra was folded into the capitalized cost. In the example, an unexplained $10 a month works out to 10 ÷ 1.06 ÷ (1/36 + 0.00125) ≈ $325.

That last step is the practical payoff of doing it by hand. The number stops being a verdict and becomes an equation, and equations can be audited.
