Insurance

Adding a driver to your policy

A car policy is priced around the people who drive, not just the car in the driveway. Change the people and the policy has questions for you, and they differ by household more than by car.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Six households follow, each adding one driver. They are illustrations, not real families, and we’ve deliberately left out any premium figures: how much a change costs depends entirely on your insurer, your state and the person being added. What the grid shows is which parts of a policy each kind of addition tends to touch, so you know what to ask before you call.

Four terms come up in every tile. Insurers use them slightly differently, so treat these as working definitions and check your own policy’s wording.

Listed driver
Someone named on the policy. The insurer has their license details and usually factors their record into the price.
Household member
Someone living with you. Many insurers expect licensed household members to be listed or formally excluded; ask how yours treats them.
Permissive use
Occasional driving by someone you allow, who isn’t on the policy. How far this stretches varies a lot.
Excluded driver
A person named as not covered. Whether this option exists at all depends on your state and insurer.

Two parents, two cars, a newly licensed teen

Who joins

A driver with no history. The insurer has nothing to go on but age and experience.

What tends to shift

Often the biggest single change a policy sees. Which car the teen is assigned to as a primary driver can matter, since some insurers rate each car by who drives it most.

Ask

Which car should the teen be assigned to? Are there discounts for grades or a driver-training course? Does anything change when they’re away at school?

A partner moves in with their own car

Who joins

An experienced driver with their own policy, their own record and, possibly, their own insurer.

What tends to shift

Two policies in one household raises the question of combining them. A clean record can help a joint policy; a rough one can cost it. Multi-car pricing may come into play.

Ask

Quote it both ways: two separate policies, and one joint policy. Does each insurer require the other person listed anyway, since you share an address?

An adult child back home for a year

Who joins

A licensed adult who may have their own car, their own policy, or neither.

What tends to shift

Living under your roof can make them a household member in the insurer’s eyes, even if they rarely touch your car. Their record now sits beside yours.

Ask

Must they be listed while living here? If they keep their own car and policy, does yours need to know? What happens on the day they move out?

A roommate who’ll borrow the car now and then

Who joins

An unrelated adult, a housemate, who expects to drive occasionally.

What tends to shift

“Occasionally” is the problem word. Permissive use may cover an infrequent borrow; regular use by someone at your address may need them listed. Insurers draw that line differently.

Ask

How often is too often before listing is required? If they’re in a crash in my car, whose policy answers first?

An older parent moves in and still drives a little

Who joins

A long-experienced driver whose driving may be tapering, possibly with their own car and policy.

What tends to shift

Often less than people fear. Annual mileage and which car they use matter. If they’ve stopped driving entirely, the conversation may be about documenting that rather than listing them.

Ask

What do you need if they no longer drive? Is there a course or program for mature drivers that affects price where we live?

A caregiver who drives the children in the family car

Who joins

Someone outside the family who drives your car regularly, on a schedule, as part of paid work.

What tends to shift

Regular use is the key fact, and so is the fact that it’s connected to employment. Both can bear on whether permissive use applies. Their record matters as much as a family member’s.

Ask

Should they be listed given regular weekly use? Does their employment by us change anything? Ask them to share their driving record before you ask the insurer.

Reading across the grid

  • The driver’s record travels with them. In every tile, the new person’s history becomes part of the policy’s arithmetic. Insurers weigh a range of factors at renewal, a subject we lay out in what moves your premium, and a new driver brings their own set of them.
  • Address matters as much as the car keys. Tiles 3 and 4 involve people who might rarely drive your car yet share your address. For many insurers that sharing is the trigger for the conversation, not the driving.
  • Frequency is the gray zone. The line between a permitted borrow and regular use isn’t fixed. If you are unsure which side a person falls on, that is itself the answer: ask, and get the reply in writing.
  • Leaving someone off to save money is a gamble on the claim, not the premium. If an unlisted driver who should have been listed has a crash, the insurer’s response is governed by your policy language and state law. You may not like finding out which way it goes.

Before you call

Have the new driver’s full name, date of birth, license number and state, and the date they’ll start driving or moved in. Know which car they’ll mostly use. If the new driver is a teen, the budget side of it, beyond insurance, is its own topic; our letter to the parent of a teen driver takes it up.

Ask for the change quoted before it’s made. Then ask one more question that most people skip: with this person added, are my liability limits still the ones I’d choose? More drivers means more chances for a serious crash, and the limits were set for the household you used to be.
