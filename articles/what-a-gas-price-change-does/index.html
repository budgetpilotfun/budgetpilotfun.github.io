Running Costs

What a gas price change does to your month

One table, two variables, forty-two cells. The cell you live in, and the one next to it, say more about next month's fuel bill than any headline.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Find your car's fuel economy down the left edge, find today's pump price along the top, and the cell where they meet is roughly what a month of gasoline costs at 1,000 miles. That is the whole tool. The rest of this page is about reading it in the three directions that matter, because a price change at the pump does not hit every household the same way.

Every number in the grid is arithmetic, not a survey: miles ÷ mpg × price per gallon, rounded to the nearest dollar. The mileage is fixed at 1,000 a month (12,000 a year) so that the only things moving are the two you can see. The prices are a spread of round figures, not a forecast of anything.

Illustrative arithmetic at 1,000 miles a month. The outlined cell — 25 mpg at $3.50 — is the reference car used in the examples below. Real consumption depends on how and where you drive, the season, and the car's condition.

Read across a row

What a price move costs your car

Stay on one row and slide right. Each 50-cent step in the pump price adds the same fixed amount to that row, and the amount is simply 500 ÷ mpg. On the 25 mpg row it is $20 a month. On the 15 mpg row it is about $33. On the 50 mpg row it is $10.

So when a headline says gas went up fifty cents, the honest answer to "what does that do to my month?" is: it depends almost entirely on the row you live in. Going from $2.50 to $5.00 — the full width of the grid — moves the 20 mpg driver from $125 to $250, an extra $125 a month or $1,500 over a year. The same move costs the 40 mpg driver $62.50 a month. Same pump, same miles, half the pain.

That is worth knowing before prices move, not after. If your row is near the top of the grid, fuel is the most volatile line in your car budget, and it deserves a cushion sized to the width of the swing you have lived through, not to the price on the day you set the budget.

Read down a column

What fuel economy is worth at a given price

Now hold the price still and move down. At $3.50, going from 15 mpg to 25 mpg saves about $93 a month ($233 to $140). Going from 25 to 35 saves $40. Going from 40 to 50 saves $17.50.

Each step down the table buys less than the one above it, because gallons used is miles divided by mpg. The early improvements remove a lot of gallons; the later ones remove a few. This is the quiet trap in comparing two efficient cars: the difference between 40 and 50 mpg sounds as large as the difference between 15 and 25, but at 1,000 miles a month it is worth a fraction as much. If one of those efficient cars costs meaningfully more to buy or insure, the fuel savings may never close the gap — and it is worth pricing that out on a per-mile basis, the way your real cost per mile walks through, before assuming the thriftier engine wins.

The reverse also holds. If you are driving something near the top of the grid, even a modest improvement is worth real money every month, and it scales directly with how far you drive.

Read on the diagonal

Where efficiency cancels out price

Look at the corners. 20 mpg at $2.50 costs $125. 40 mpg at $5.00 also costs $125. Double the price and double the efficiency, and the monthly bill does not move. The same pattern runs straight through the middle of the table: the $100 cell appears five times — 25 mpg at $2.50, 30 at $3.00, 35 at $3.50, 40 at $4.00 and 50 at $5.00. In each pair, the price and the efficiency have risen by the same proportion.

The diagonal is useful for one practical question: how much would prices have to rise before a more efficient car leaves me no worse off than I am today? Find your current cell, then look for the same number further down and to the right. That pair tells you, in plain terms, how much protection the efficiency buys.

Rescale for your own miles

If you don't drive 1,000 a month

Every cell is proportional to mileage, so adjusting it is a single multiplication:

your monthly cost = cell value × (your monthly miles ÷ 1,000)

At 1,500 miles a month, the reference car's $140 becomes $210. At 600 miles, it becomes $84. A 50-cent price step on that row, which was $20 at 1,000 miles, becomes $30 at 1,500 and $12 at 600. The shape of the grid does not change; only its scale does. Someone who drives twice as far lives on a grid where every cell, and every price shock, is doubled.

If you have not tracked your actual monthly mileage, two odometer readings a month apart are enough to start. Fill-up receipts will also tell you your real mpg, which is often different from the figure on the window sticker. The window figure is a standardized test result; your number reflects your commute, your climate, and your right foot.

Outside the frame

What the grid leaves out

The table is deliberately narrow. It ignores a few things that can move the real number:

  • Seasonal economy. Many cars use more fuel in cold weather and with the air conditioning running hard. Your row can drift up and down during the year.
  • Grade. If your owner's manual calls for premium, your price column is the premium price, not the one on the big sign.
  • Mileage that responds to price. When gas is expensive, some trips get combined or skipped, which pulls the bill back down. The grid assumes you drive the same regardless.
  • Everything else per mile. Fuel is one line. Tires, maintenance and depreciation also scale with miles, and they don't show up in a gas grid at all.

For an electric car the same structure applies with different units — kWh per 100 miles instead of mpg, cents per kWh instead of dollars per gallon. That version, with a small calculator, is in what charging an EV at home costs.

One last habit makes the grid genuinely useful: pencil your own cell onto it, then the cell one column to the right. The distance between them is what the next price rise will cost you, and it is a number you can set aside before it arrives.
