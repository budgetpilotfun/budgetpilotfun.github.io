Leasing

Ending a lease early

A lease is built to be finished. Leaving early is possible, but every exit has a price, and the prices are calculated differently.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

The menu below lists the usual ways out of a consumer car lease before its scheduled end. The prices beside each are descriptions, not figures, because each is calculated from your contract's terms and the car's value on the day. To make the mechanics concrete, every entry is worked against the same illustrative lease: twenty months into a thirty-six month term, with sixteen payments of $420 still to go, a payoff figure from the lessor of $24,500, and a car the market would pay somewhere around $22,000 to $23,500 for. None of those numbers belong to anyone; they are there to show how the arithmetic runs.

The exit menu

Prices vary with the market and the contract

Early terminationoften the steepest

Hand the car back to the lessor and ask to be released.

  • How it is priced: leases commonly compute an early termination charge from the gap between what you still owe under the contract and what the car is worth to the lessor, plus an early termination fee, plus any unpaid amounts and taxes. The method is set out in the early termination section of your lease disclosures.
  • Why it runs high: the car's value used in that calculation may be a wholesale figure, not what you could sell it for, and early in a lease the balance owed is usually far above either.
  • Watch for: mileage and wear charges, which may still apply at an early return.

Illustrative: 24,500 owed − 22,000 value = 2,500
+ early termination fee (whatever the contract states)
+ any wear or mileage charges and taxes

Lease transferfees, plus a stranger

Someone else takes over your payments for the rest of the term.

  • How it is priced: a transfer fee from the lessor, a listing fee if you use a marketplace, and sometimes a cash incentive you offer to make your lease attractive.
  • The mechanics: the lessor must allow it and must approve the incoming person. Some do not permit transfers at all; some limit them by state or by account age.
  • Watch for: whether you stay liable if the new lessee stops paying. The transfer paperwork, not a marketplace's summary, answers that. The whole process has its own page, taking over someone else's lease.

Illustrative: no gap to cover; your cost is the fees
and any incentive, while the $6,720 of remaining
payments (16 × 420) moves to someone else.

Trade it in at a dealermarket price, today

A dealer pays off the lease and takes the car, usually as part of selling you another.

  • How it is priced: the dealer's offer against the lessor's payoff. If the offer is higher, the difference is equity you can take or apply. If it is lower, you owe the gap.
  • The mechanics: some lessors restrict which dealers can buy their cars out of a lease, or charge them differently. Ask the lessor before you shop.
  • Watch for: a shortfall rolled quietly into the next car's price. It does not disappear there; it just starts collecting interest or rent. Rolling negative equity into the next loan shows where that leads.

Illustrative: offer 22,800 − payoff 24,500
= 1,700 you owe, in cash or folded into the next deal.

Buy it out earlypayoff, plus tax

Pay the lessor's payoff figure and own the car, then keep it or sell it yourself.

  • How it is priced: the payoff quote from the lessor, any purchase-option fee, and the sales tax and title fees your state applies to the purchase.
  • The mechanics: once it is yours, there are no mileage or wear charges to face. Selling privately may fetch more than a dealer would pay, but you handle the sale and carry the car until it sells.
  • Watch for: taxes on the buyout itself, which vary by state, and the time it takes for a title to arrive before you can sell.

Illustrative: pay 24,500 + tax and fees, sell privately
for 23,500 → about 1,000 short, plus those taxes and fees.

House option: stay putthe remaining payments

Keep paying and leave on schedule.

Illustrative: 16 × 420 = $6,720, plus end-of-lease
fees, for sixteen more months of driving.

Ordering from it

Start with the house option, because it is the baseline every other choice is measured against. Staying put costs $6,720 in the example, but that money buys sixteen months of a car. An early termination that costs $2,500 plus a fee buys you nothing except freedom, and then you still need transport from somewhere. Compare exits on what you would pay and what you would have afterwards.

Next, get the one number every exit depends on: the lessor's payoff figure, in writing, with a good-through date. Then find out what the car is worth, using more than one offer. With those two numbers the menu mostly prices itself. If the market value is above the payoff, several exits suddenly cost little or even pay you, and when your leased car is worth more than the payoff explains how to capture it. If the market value is well below, the transfer route becomes more interesting, because it is the only one that does not require someone to cover the gap.

Finally, read the contract's early termination section before you call. The disclosure explains how the charge is calculated; knowing the method lets you check the figure you are quoted. The Federal Trade Commission's consumer pages on vehicle leasing, at consumer.ftc.gov, are a reasonable plain-language companion to that section.

When to wait instead

Early-exit costs often shrink as a lease ages, because the balance owed falls toward the residual while the car's value falls more slowly than it did in the first year. If the reason for leaving is a preference rather than a crisis, a few more months can make a costly exit much cheaper. If the reason is a job loss or a move abroad, call the lessor before you miss anything; a conversation held while you are current goes very differently from one held after.

The cheapest dish on this menu is often the one you already ordered. Not always, though, which is why it is worth reading the rest.
