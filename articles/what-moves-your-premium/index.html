Insurance

What moves your premium at renewal

The renewal price arrives as one number, but it is the reading on a whole instrument panel. Some of the dials are yours to turn. Most of the others move on their own.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

A renewal notice rarely explains itself. It shows last term's price, this term's price and, if you are lucky, a line saying rates have changed. Behind that one number sit a dozen or so inputs, some of which you set, many of which you never touch. Sorting them into those two groups is the first useful thing to do with a price increase, because it tells you whether to change your policy or to change your insurer.

Each dial below shows how much say you have over that factor, not how much it moves your price. That second question has no general answer; insurers weight things differently, and we will not pretend to know their formulas. The readings are our editorial judgment, meant to be argued with.

Dials you control

bank one · 7 factors
most

Coverages and limits

The single largest lever on the bill, and entirely yours. Every coverage on the declarations page has a price, and some may be doing nothing for you. The one place not to economize is liability, which protects everything you own.

most

Deductibles

Raising them lowers the premium; whether that is a good trade is a matter of arithmetic, which choosing a deductible sets out. The dial is high because you can change it with one phone call.

a lot

Which car you insure

Repair costs, theft appeal and how a model tends to fare in claims all feed the price. The control is real but it is exercised at purchase, not at renewal. Get a quote before you buy, not after.

some

Miles you report

Many insurers ask how far you drive, and fewer miles can mean less exposure. Update the figure when your life changes, and keep it truthful. Some insurers also offer programs that measure driving directly, which trades some privacy for a possible discount.

some

Who is on the policy

Every listed driver brings their own age, experience and record. Adding a new driver, or removing one who has moved out and insured their own car, can shift the price in either direction.

some

Discounts you ask about

Insurers keep their own lists, and not every applicable discount is applied automatically. Asking once a year which ones you qualify for costs nothing. The dial sits mid-way because the menu is theirs.

a little

How you pay

Some insurers charge a fee for monthly installments or price a paid-in-full term differently. Whether yours does is on the billing page or a question away.

Dials you don't

bank two · 6 factors
barely

Claim costs where you live

Local repair labor, parts, medical costs, theft, weather and how often claims end up in court all shape the base rate for your area. You influence this only by moving.

none

Your insurer's rate changes

Insurers revise their rates for everyone in a class at once, and in many states those changes go through a regulator first. When the whole book goes up, your renewal goes up with it.

slowly

Your record so far

Tickets and at-fault claims stay on your record for a period the insurer and state rules decide. You cannot edit the past, but every clean month is a vote for the future, which is why this dial isn't at zero.

time only

Age and years licensed

Experience is priced, and nothing but the calendar supplies it. This factor can help at renewal without you doing anything at all.

slowly

Credit-based insurance score

Where it is allowed, some insurers use a score built from credit information. Some states limit or bar the practice. The score moves slowly, which is why this sits low; why credit can affect insurance goes further.

barely

Your car getting older

An aging car is usually worth less, which can reduce what collision and comprehensive could pay out. Repair costs, though, don't fall on the same schedule, so the savings are often smaller than people expect.

Reading the panel on renewal day

Put last year's declarations page next to this year's. Anything that changed in bank one is visible there: a coverage added, a driver listed, a vehicle swapped, a discount that fell off. If you find the change, you have found at least part of the reason, and you can decide whether to reverse it.

If every line in bank one is identical and the price still rose, the movement came from bank two. That is not a verdict on you as a driver. It usually means costs rose across a whole group of policyholders at once. You cannot turn those dials, but you can take the same set of inputs to other insurers, who read bank two differently. That is precisely the situation where comparing quotes pays off, and shopping your policy at renewal gives you a script and a sheet for it.

It is worth asking your current insurer directly what drove the change. Some will tell you in plain terms, some will point to a general rate revision, and some will not say much. Any answer narrows the search.

One caution about bank one. The dials that lower a premium fastest are coverage and deductible, and those are also the two that decide how much a bad day costs you. Turning them down to offset a bank-two increase feels like a fix and is really a transfer of risk from the insurer back onto your savings. Sometimes that transfer is sensible. It should be a decision, not a reflex to a bigger number on an envelope.
