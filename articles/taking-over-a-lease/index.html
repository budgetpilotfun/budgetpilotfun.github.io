Leasing

Taking over someone else’s lease

Two people, one contract, fourteen months left on it. What follows is the conversation they should have before anyone signs a transfer form.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read
Outgoing: holds the lease, wants out Incoming: wants a car without a three-year commitment

This is a composite conversation, not a transcript of real people. Every figure in it is illustrative. The shape of the negotiation is the point.

Outgoing

Fourteen months left at $389 a month. You pick up the payments, I hand you the keys, done. It’s the easiest car you’ll ever get.

Incoming

Maybe. Before we talk price: does your leasing company even allow this?

That question isn’t settled between the two of them. Some lessors permit a transfer (often called an assumption), some limit it to applicants who pass their own credit review, some restrict where the car can be registered afterward, and some don’t allow it at all. The agreement usually has a clause headed something like “assignment” or “transfer”, and the lessor’s customer line can say what the process currently is.

Outgoing

I called. They allow it. You’d fill out their application, and there’s a transfer fee.

Incoming

Who pays it?

Outgoing

I was hoping you would.

Incoming

And I was hoping you would. So that goes on the list of things we negotiate, not things we assume.

Whether there is a fee, how much it is and when it’s due are set by the lessor. Who ends up paying it is purely a matter of what the two parties agree to. Write it down either way.

Incoming

Let’s price what I’d be taking on. Fourteen payments of $389.

14 × $389 = $5,446
Outgoing

That’s the whole thing.

Incoming

It’s the part you can see. There’s also whatever it costs to hand the car back at the end: an end-of-lease fee if the contract has one, plus anything charged for wear or extra miles. None of that is yours anymore once I sign. It’s mine.

This is the piece the outgoing side almost always leaves out. The person stepping in inherits the back end of the lease: the turn-in inspection, the mileage reckoning, and any disposition fee written into the contract. They are paying for months of use they get, and for a return they didn’t cause most of.

Incoming

Miles, then. What’s the allowance, and what does the odometer say?

Outgoing

Twelve thousand a year on a thirty-six-month lease, so 36,000 total. It’s at 25,000. I’m twenty-two months in.

Incoming

At twenty-two months an even pace would be 22,000, so you’re 3,000 ahead. That leaves me 11,000 miles for fourteen months. I drive about a thousand a month.

36,000 − 25,000 = 11,000 left  ·  11,000 ÷ 14 ≈ 786 a month
25,000 + (14 × 1,000) = 39,000 → 3,000 over
Outgoing

So you’d go over.

Incoming

By about 3,000, at my pace. I need the per-mile charge from the contract itself. If it were 20 cents, that’s $600 I’d owe on the way out.

3,000 × $0.20 = $600 (illustrative rate)

The per-mile rate is printed in the lease; nobody should have to guess it. Twenty cents is a placeholder, not a typical figure. For projecting your own overage, see going over your lease miles.

Outgoing

I could put cash toward it. People who advertise a lease for transfer often offer an incentive to whoever takes it.

Incoming

Then let’s size the incentive to the problem instead of picking a round number. Say you put in $1,000 and my projected overage stays at $600.

$5,446 + $600 − $1,000 = $5,046
$5,046 ÷ 14 ≈ $360 a month, before any transfer fee, disposition fee or wear charges
Incoming

That’s the number I compare against other ways of getting a car for a year and change. Not $389, and not $360 either, until we know the fees.

Outgoing

Fair. And once you sign, I’m out. Right?

Incoming

You should ask them that in exactly those words.

Whether the original lessee is fully released or stays on the hook if the new person stops paying depends on the lessor and on the transfer documents. Some transfers are a clean release; others leave the outgoing person secondarily liable. Ask the lessor directly, get the answer in writing, and read the release language in the transfer agreement before signing it. If the answer is “you remain liable,” the incoming person’s reliability is suddenly your problem too.

Incoming

Condition next. I want the service records, and I want a mechanic I choose to look at it. And we both photograph every panel, wheel and seat on handover day, with the date on the files.

Outgoing

You think I’m hiding something?

Incoming

I think in fourteen months an inspector is going to walk around this car and send a bill to whoever holds the lease. That will be me. The scuff on the rear bumper happened on your watch.

What the inspection treats as normal versus excess is set by the lease’s wear standard, which varies. Reading it before the handover tells both sides which marks matter and which don’t. Repairing something now, or pricing it into the incentive, are both reasonable; ignoring it just moves the cost to the end.

Incoming

Two more. I’ll need my own insurance that meets whatever coverage the lease requires, in force before I take the car. And if I register it somewhere other than where you did, I need to know the lessor allows that and what my motor vehicle agency wants.

Lease agreements typically spell out required coverage limits and name the lessor on the policy; the specifics vary, so they come from the contract rather than from memory. Insuring a leased car walks through what to look for. Registration and titling rules are set state by state.

Outgoing

Anything else?

Incoming

The purchase option. If I end up liking the car, is the buyout price in the contract the same one I’d get?

The purchase option price is written into the original agreement. Ask whether it carries over to the new lessee unchanged, and whether the transfer adds anything to it.

Outgoing

So where does that leave us?

Incoming

Three open items. Who pays the transfer fee. How much cash you put in, sized to the miles and the bumper. And a written answer on whether you’re released. Bring me those and I’ll fill out their application the same day.

Notice who did most of the asking. The person leaving a lease is thinking about the payments they stop making; the person taking it over has to think about the months they’ll drive and the day they’ll hand it back. Both are reading the same contract. Only one of them is reading all of it.
