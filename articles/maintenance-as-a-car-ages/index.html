Running Costs

Budgeting maintenance as a car ages

A car's upkeep has a plot: a quiet opening, a busy middle, and an ending full of decisions. Each chapter calls for a different way of setting money aside.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Cars don't age evenly. They go through long quiet stretches and then, all at once, a cluster of parts reaches the end of its design life together. A maintenance budget that sets aside the same amount every month for the whole of a car's life will be too generous early and too thin later. It is more useful to think of a car's life in chapters.

The four below are loose. Where one ends and the next begins depends on the make, the miles, the climate and how the car has been treated, and your owner's manual — specifically its maintenance schedule — is the authority on intervals. We've kept dollar figures out on purpose: prices for the same job vary widely by car and by region, and a national average would tell you nothing about your own bill. The method at the end of each chapter is how to build your number from your own records instead.

I
Chapter one

The early years

A new or nearly new car mostly asks for routine service: oil and filter changes, tire rotations, inspections, a cabin or engine air filter now and then. If the car is still under a manufacturer's warranty, many defects that do appear are the manufacturer's problem, not yours — though what a warranty covers, for how long and on what conditions is spelled out in the warranty booklet, and it differs from maker to maker.

What tends to come due

  • Scheduled oil service and rotations at the manual's intervals
  • Filters, wiper blades, washer fluid
  • Possibly the first set of replacement tires, if the miles are high
  • Recall work, normally performed free of charge through the manufacturer's dealers

The quiet here is a trap of its own. Because the bills are small, it is easy to conclude that the car is cheap to run and to size the budget accordingly. The real job of these years is to bank money for chapters three and four while nothing is asking for it.

Keep every receipt from the start. A complete service history is what lets you forecast the later chapters, and it helps when the car is eventually sold.

Budgeting method: price the scheduled services listed in your manual for the next two years with a local shop, add them up, divide by 24, and then deliberately save more than that figure. The surplus is the start of the fund later chapters will draw on.

II
Chapter two

The middle years

Somewhere past the warranty, the car starts consuming wear items in earnest. None of this means anything is wrong; these are parts designed to be used up. But they are larger and lumpier than an oil change, and the first time several arrive in the same year is often when owners first feel the car has become expensive.

What tends to come due

  • Brake pads, and sometimes rotors
  • A 12-volt battery
  • Tires, if they haven't been replaced already
  • Larger scheduled services: spark plugs, certain fluids, belts — whatever your manual lists at these mileages
  • Suspension and steering parts beginning to show wear

This is the chapter where the service menu at a quick-lube counter starts suggesting fluid flushes, and where it pays to know which ones your manual calls for. The service menu, translated goes through the common ones line by line.

Budgeting method: list the wear items above for your car, note from your receipts when each was last replaced, and estimate when each will come due next, by miles or by years. Get a quote for each. Spread each quote over the months until it is due. The result is uneven by design — a monthly amount that rises as more items approach.

III
Chapter three

The late years

Now repairs join maintenance. Parts that were never on a schedule — they were expected to last a long time, not forever — begin to fail. The distinction matters for budgeting. Maintenance can be predicted from the manual. Repairs can only be anticipated in general terms, which means the budget has to shift from a schedule to a reserve.

What tends to come due

  • Rubber and plastic parts: hoses, bushings, mounts, seals
  • Cooling-system components: water pump, radiator, thermostat
  • Starter or alternator
  • Exhaust parts, and sensors that feed the engine computer
  • Timing belt replacement, on engines that use one, at the interval the manual gives
  • Rust-related work in climates that salt the roads

The car may be paid off by now, which is the reason these years can be the cheapest of ownership overall even as the repair bills grow. That only works if the money that used to go to a payment is still going somewhere — ideally into the reserve.

Budgeting method: keep the chapter-two schedule for wear items, and add a standing reserve sized to the largest single repair you would choose to pay for rather than replace the car. How big a car repair fund should be builds that figure step by step from your own car's facts.

IV
Chapter four

The very old car

A very old car is less a maintenance problem than a series of decisions. Each significant repair invites the same question: is this car worth this money? Mechanically, many cars can be kept going for a very long time; financially, the answer depends on what the car is worth, what the repair costs, and what replacing it would cost instead.

What tends to come due

  • Anything from the earlier chapters, again
  • Major components: transmission, engine internals, air conditioning compressor
  • Parts availability problems, which raise cost and time off the road
  • Inspection or emissions failures, where your state requires them

One common rule of thumb compares a repair's cost with the car's market value, but it misses what matters. A car worth little can still be the cheapest transport available if it is otherwise sound, because the alternative is buying another car and inheriting its unknown history. The better comparison is a repair's cost against the months of reliable use it buys, set beside what those same months would cost in a replacement. When a cheap car turns out expensive charts that race over five years.

Budgeting method: for any repair above your reserve, write down three numbers — the repair quote, your realistic estimate of how many more months the car will run well afterward, and what a replacement would cost you per month over the same period. Divide the first by the second and compare the result with the third.

Read together, the four chapters argue for a budget that climbs with the car's age while the savings from early ownership carry it through. The owner who treats chapter one's small bills as the permanent cost of the car is the one surprised by chapter three. The owner who saved through chapter one usually isn't — and gets to make chapter four's decisions on the merits rather than under pressure.
