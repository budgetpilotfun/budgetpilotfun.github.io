Borrowing

Reading a retail installment contract

Put the contract on the table before anything else gets signed. Below is a specimen, simplified and filled with illustrative numbers, with a numbered marker on each part worth stopping at.

  1. Who you're actually borrowing from

    A dealer-arranged loan usually starts as a contract between you and the dealer, who is listed as the seller-creditor. Most are then sold, or assigned, to a bank or finance company (marker 11). So the name at the top may not be the name on your statements. Ask who the contract is expected to go to, because that company's payment rules are the ones you'll live with.

  2. The APR, the one rate that counts

    The Truth in Lending disclosures, the boxed row, are required by federal law in consumer credit contracts, and the APR is the headline. It folds the interest and certain finance-related costs into one yearly figure, so it is the number to compare between offers. If it differs from what you were told verbally, stop there. The contract governs, not the conversation.

  3. The finance charge in dollars

    $5,129.40 here is the total price of borrowing if you pay exactly on schedule. It is the total of payments minus the amount financed: $29,129.40 − $24,000.00. Say that figure out loud before signing. It lands differently than a percentage does.

  4. The amount financed, and what's hiding in it

    This is not the price of the car. It is everything you are borrowing, which in the specimen includes a service contract and a gap waiver (marker 9). Trace this number to the itemization and make sure every line in it is something you agreed to.

  5. Total of payments

    Sixty payments of $485.49 is $29,129.40. Multiply the payment by the number of payments yourself; if your result doesn't match the box, ask why before anything else. On real contracts the last payment is sometimes a few cents different, which is normal. A gap of dollars isn't.

  6. Total sale price

    The whole cost of buying this car on these terms: the total of payments plus the $3,000 down. $32,129.40 is the number to hold against the $21,900 vehicle price you negotiated, and the distance between them is taxes, fees, add-ons and interest.

  7. The payment schedule

    Check the number of payments, the amount and the first due date. A first payment due sooner or later than you expect changes how much interest accrues before it. Put the date in your calendar the day you sign; lenders don't always send a first bill in time.

  8. Late charges, prepayment, security

    Three short lines with long consequences. The late charge terms and any grace period are set by the contract and limited in different ways by different states, so read your own, not a general rule. The prepayment line tells you whether paying early is penalty-free and how interest is calculated, which decides whether an early payoff saves what you expect (see paying a car loan off early). The security line means the car is collateral: miss enough payments and it can be repossessed.

  9. The itemization, line by line

    The most useful block on the page. Cash price, then taxes and fees, then "amounts paid to others," which is where optional products usually appear. Our specimen's tax and title figures are placeholders; yours depend on your state and are worth confirming with its motor vehicle agency or revenue department. Every dollar in this list is financed at the APR for the full term. The add-ons that end up inside your loan shows what that does to a $1,000 product.

  10. Optional means optional

    Service contracts, gap waivers, protection packages: contracts commonly say these are not required to obtain credit, with a separate signature for each. If you were told a product was required, and the contract says it isn't, believe the contract and ask for it to be removed before signing. Also look for how each can be cancelled later, and whether any unused portion is refunded.

  11. Assignment

    Once assigned, the finance company holds the contract and you deal with it directly. Some sales are also written as conditional on that assignment happening. If your paperwork says delivery depends on the dealer finding a buyer for the contract, ask what happens, and to your down payment and trade-in, if it doesn't.

  12. The pages nobody reads

    The back pages set the rules on default, insurance you must carry, where disputes are heard, and more. An arbitration clause, if present, may limit your ability to go to court. None of that is unusual, but it is binding, and you are allowed to take the time to read it. A seller who objects to you reading the contract has told you something useful.

Take a copy of everything you sign, complete and with every blank filled in, before you leave with the car. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau keeps plain-language material on auto financing at consumerfinance.gov if you want a second reader's view of any section.
