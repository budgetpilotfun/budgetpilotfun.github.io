Leasing

The disposition fee

It is usually the smallest line on the final lease bill and somehow the one people remember. A single fee, looked at from every side, and nothing else.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Disposition fee

Also called
Turn-in fee, return fee, vehicle return fee, termination fee (in some contracts)
Paid to
The lessor, the company that owns the car
Triggered by
Handing the car back instead of buying it, in contracts that include one
Amount
Printed in your lease. Varies by lessor and contract; don’t rely on a figure you heard elsewhere
Found under
End-of-term obligations, return of vehicle, or the fee schedule in the disclosures

What

A flat charge for taking the car back. When a leased car is returned, the lessor has to receive it, inspect it, clean it up, transport it and resell it, usually at auction or through a dealer. The disposition fee is the lessor’s way of recovering some of that handling cost from the person who chose to return rather than keep the car.

It is not a penalty for damage, and it is not a mileage charge. Those are separate lines, calculated separately, and they can appear on the same final bill. Keeping the three apart in your head is most of the battle.

When

At the end, if you return. The fee is normally assessed on the final statement that follows the turn-in, along with any wear and mileage charges and any payments still owed.

If the lease ends early, the fee may be folded into the early-termination calculation rather than appearing on its own. The contract’s early-termination section shows how.

Why not

Because the fee exists to cover reselling a returned car, contracts commonly don’t charge it when you buy the car at lease end: nothing is being disposed of. Buying out tends to bring its own charge instead, often called a purchase option fee, which is a different line with a different amount. Check both in your agreement; don’t assume one replaces the other.

Some lessors also waive the disposition fee if you lease or buy another vehicle through them, or offer to under certain conditions. Whether yours does, and what the conditions are, is a question for the lessor, not something to infer from a friend’s lease with a different company.

Where

Search the agreement for “disposition” first. If nothing comes up, try “return”, “turn-in” and “end of term”. The amount is usually stated as a fixed dollar figure. If you can’t find it, the lessor’s customer service can quote it from your contract, and it is worth asking them to confirm by email or letter.

Before signing a new lease, the fee is worth finding on the paperwork while you can still ask about it. Whether it can be changed at that stage varies; afterward, it is just a number you agreed to.

Budget

The fee is known the day you sign, which means it never has to be a surprise. Spread it across the term and it is a small monthly set-aside. Using an illustrative fee of $395 on a 36-month lease:

$395 ÷ 36 months ≈ $10.97 a month

Eleven dollars a month into a separate pot, and the final bill’s smallest line is already covered. Swap in your own contract’s figure and term.

Choices

The fee is one of the few end-of-lease numbers that nudges the return-or-buy decision in a predictable direction. Suppose the contract’s disposition fee is $395 and its purchase option fee is $300 (both illustrative). Returning costs the first; buying costs the second plus the buyout price. On its own that gap is too small to decide anything, and it should never be the reason to buy a car you don’t want. But when the other numbers are close, it belongs in the comparison, alongside any wear and mileage charges that buying would avoid.

Return: $395 + wear charges + mileage charges
Buy: $300 + purchase price (+ taxes and title fees where they apply)

The full comparison lives in your options at lease end.

Transfers

If a lease changes hands, the fee travels with the contract. Whoever holds the lease at the end is the one who returns the car, and so the one who pays. A person taking over a lease should count it as part of what they’re taking on.

Proof

Because the fee is billed after the car is gone, the paperwork from turn-in day is what you will lean on if anything on the final statement looks wrong. Keep the turn-in receipt with its date and odometer figure, your own photos of the car at handover, and any written confirmation of a waiver. If the fee on the statement doesn’t match the figure in your agreement, or appears after you were told in writing it would be waived, that is a billing question with a clear answer, and the documents make it a short call rather than a long one. The federal Consumer Financial Protection Bureau at consumerfinance.gov takes complaints if a lessor won’t engage.

Traps

Three ways the fee catches people out:

Confusing it with damage. A final bill with several lines looks like one charge. Read each line separately. The disposition fee should match the contract exactly; the wear charges are where disputes, if any, belong. The wear-and-tear standard is a different conversation.

Assuming a waiver. “They’ll waive it if you get another car” may be true of some lessors, some of the time. Get it in writing before you sign the next deal, not after.

Forgetting it exists. It arrives weeks after the car has gone, when the lease already feels finished. That is the whole reason to set the money aside early.

A disposition fee is a known cost wearing the costume of a surprise. Find the number, divide it by the months, and it stops being either.
