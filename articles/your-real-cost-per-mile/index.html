Your real cost per mile

Print this page, or copy the lines onto the back of an envelope. Fill in the boxes on the left from your own paperwork; the margin shows one illustrative car worked through, so you can check your method against ours.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Cost per mile is the most portable number in car budgeting. It lets you compare a commute against a transit pass, price a road trip honestly, or decide whether a second car earns its keep. Most people only ever see the fuel part of it. The sheet below gathers everything else and divides by the same miles.

Use one year as the period. Where a cost is lumpy — tires, a big repair — spread it over the years it covers. The worked example is an invented car and household with round numbers; none of its figures are averages or typical costs, just inputs for the arithmetic.

Two numbers, two jobs

The sheet ends with two answers on purpose. Line K is the average cost: everything the car consumed in a year, divided across every mile. In the example it's about 63 cents. Line L is the marginal cost: what an extra mile adds, given that the car is already sitting in the driveway, insured and registered. In the example it's about 18 cents.

Use K when the question is whether to own this car at all. If a household could cover its 12,000 miles another way for less than $7,592 a year, the car is costing more than its job is worth. Use L when the car is a settled fact and the question is a single trip: driving 300 miles to a family event costs this household roughly $54 in wear and fuel ($0.18 × 300 is $53.80), not the $190 that the average figure would suggest.

The two figures get confused all the time, usually in the direction that flatters the car. Comparing a train fare to fuel alone uses a marginal cost that ignores wear. Comparing a rideshare bill to the full 63 cents overstates what you'd save by leaving the car at home once, because insurance and depreciation carry on regardless. Pick the number that matches the decision.

Where the sheet bends

Depreciation is partly per-mile. A car loses value with age and with miles. Treating all of G as fixed, as line L does, slightly understates the marginal cost of a high-mileage year. If you drive far more than usual, move some of G into L.

Maintenance is partly fixed. Some services fall due by the calendar rather than the odometer. The owner's manual says which.

The IRS rate is a different animal. The IRS publishes a standard mileage rate each year for business, medical and some other uses of a personal car. It's set for tax purposes across all vehicles and is not a measurement of your car. For its current value and the rules on using it, see irs.gov; for how car costs interact with taxes more broadly, car expenses and taxes: the basics points to where to confirm the details.

Two cars, two sheets. A household with two cars should fill in one sheet per car. Averaging them together hides the fact that one car is often far more expensive per mile than the other, which is exactly the fact that tells you which one to drive for the long trips.

Filled in once, the sheet is a snapshot. Filled in each year on the same date, it becomes a record of how the car's economics shift as it ages — depreciation shrinking, maintenance growing — which is the evidence you'll want when deciding when to trade in.
