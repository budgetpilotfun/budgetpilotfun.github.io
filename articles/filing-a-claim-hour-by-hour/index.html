Insurance

Filing a claim, hour by hour

This log belongs to nobody. It is a composite of an ordinary rear-end collision, assembled to show the sequence and the paperwork, not one real person's claim.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read
FILE: composite claim, property damage + minor injury · not-at-fault · rear-end, low speed · all times and names illustrative
at the sceneon the phoneat the shoppaperworkhome or waiting
  1. Day 15:47 p.m.
    Impact. Stopped at a light, waiting to turn left, the car is hit from behind at low speed. No airbags. The rear bumper is pushed in and the trunk lid no longer sits flush.
  2. Day 15:48 p.m.
    Check people first. Everyone in both cars is asked whether they are hurt. Nobody reports pain yet, which is noted rather than trusted; soreness can surface a day or two later. Hazard lights on.
  3. Day 15:52 p.m.
    Call, if it fits. Traffic is moving around both cars and nobody is injured, so the call is to the non-emergency line. Whether police come to a minor crash differs a lot from place to place. The dispatcher gives an incident number either way, and it goes in the notes.
  4. Day 15:56 p.m.
    Photographs. Wide shots of both cars in position, then close-ups of every damaged panel on both vehicles, the other car's plate, the intersection, the light, the road surface. Forty-odd photos. Space on a phone is cheap; a missing angle is not.
  5. Day 16:04 p.m.
    Exchange details. Name, phone, insurer, policy number, plate, and a photo of the other driver's license and insurance card. The other driver says they looked down at their phone. That is written down word for word. Fault is not argued; it isn't decided at the curb.
  6. Day 16:12 p.m.
    Witness. A pedestrian saw it. Name and number, and one sentence of what they saw, typed into the phone while they are still standing there.
  7. Day 17:35 p.m.
    Notes, while fresh. A plain account of the sequence, times, weather and what was said, written the same evening. Details fade faster than anyone expects.
  8. Day 28:40 a.m.
    Own insurer notified. Most policies ask you to report an accident promptly, even one you did not cause. The claim is logged as not-at-fault for now. The agent explains the two routes: claim against the other driver's liability coverage, or use this policy's collision coverage and let the insurer recover the money later.
  9. Day 29:15 a.m.
    Claim opened with their insurer. Claim number recorded. The adjuster will contact the other driver to confirm their account before accepting responsibility. Rental is not authorized until that happens.
  10. Day 3—
    Neck stiffness. A mild ache appears. A doctor's visit is booked and both insurers are told, because an injury changes how a claim is handled. Nothing about injuries is signed or settled at this stage.
  11. Day 511:20 a.m.
    Liability accepted. The other insurer accepts fault on behalf of its driver. A rental is authorized, with a daily cap and a note that it runs for a reasonable repair period. The cap is written down.
  12. Day 6—
    Estimate. The insurer suggests a shop from its network; the owner prefers an independent shop they trust. How much choice you have here depends on your state and your policy, and it is worth asking before agreeing to anything. The first estimate is for visible damage only.
  13. Day 11—
    Supplement. With the bumper off, the shop finds a bent reinforcement bar and a cracked sensor bracket. It sends a supplement to the insurer, which sends an adjuster back out. Two days lost, which is normal and is why first estimates are rarely final.
  14. Day 194:30 p.m.
    Pick-up. Before signing for the car: panel gaps checked, paint matched in daylight, every warning light off, trunk opened and closed, the backup camera tested. A written copy of the repair warranty goes in the file.
  15. Day 21—
    The lost-value question. A repaired car can be worth less than one that was never damaged. Whether a claim for that loss can be made against the at-fault driver's insurer depends on the state and the circumstances. The question is asked, in writing. The answer is recorded, whatever it is.
  16. Day 34—
    Settlement. The property side closes: repairs paid directly to the shop, rental paid to the rental company. The injury side stays open until treatment is finished. A release arrives for the injury claim. It is not signed yet; a release is final, and it waits until the doctor says the neck is done.

What the log is really recording

Read down the timestamps and a pattern shows. The first half hour carries most of the weight. Almost everything that later decides how smoothly the claim runs, from the photographs to the witness to the other driver's own words, was gathered before 6:15 on the first evening. The following month is mostly waiting, punctuated by phone calls in which somebody asks for a number you either wrote down or didn't.

The second pattern is that nothing happened in one step. Fault took several days to accept. The repair estimate grew once the bumper came off. The injury side outlived the property side by weeks. None of that is a sign that something went wrong; it is how a routine claim looks from the inside. Expecting it makes the wait less alarming.

The fork on Day 2

The entry at 8:40 a.m. mentions two routes, and they are worth a closer look. Claiming against the at-fault driver's insurer avoids your deductible, but you move at their speed, and nothing moves until they accept liability. Claiming through your own collision coverage is often quicker to start, but you pay your deductible up front; your insurer may then pursue the other side for its costs and your deductible, a process usually called subrogation, and refund you if it succeeds. Whether a not-at-fault claim affects your own premium is an insurer-by-insurer question, so ask before choosing. The size of that deductible, and whether you'd want it lower, is its own calculation in choosing a deductible.

If the other driver had no insurance, or not enough, the log would branch differently on Day 2; that situation is laid out in uninsured motorist coverage.

A page to keep in the glove box

Everything in the first seven entries fits on an index card: check for injuries, make the call that fits, photograph everything, exchange details and photograph their documents, get witnesses, say little about fault, write it all down the same day. The rest of the log follows from having done those. A claim is, in the end, an argument made with records, and the person with the better records usually has the easier month.
