Asking for the out-the-door price

The price in the ad is where the arithmetic starts, not where it ends. Here is one illustrative climb, bar by bar.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

$22,500 is the price in the ad. Nobody who buys that car will pay $22,500 for it. They will pay the advertised price plus whatever the dealership, the state and the county add on the way to the door, and that final figure is the one that matters. It has a name: the out-the-door price.

The chart below follows one illustrative deal from the ad to the total. Every amount in it is invented for the example. Real fees and tax rates depend on where you live and register the car, and on the dealer, so treat the shape as the lesson, not the numbers.

Four steps added $3,455.64, a little over 15% on top of the advertised figure. None of those steps is unusual, and none is necessarily improper. But only one of them, the first, appeared in the ad.

Each step, in order

  1. The advertised price

    The starting bar. Read the ad's small print before treating it as real: some advertised prices assume a rebate you may not qualify for, a trade-in, or financing through the dealer. If the fine print lists conditions, the figure you can actually buy at may be higher before anything else is added.

  2. Dealer-installed add-ons

    Things like door-edge guards, wheel locks, nitrogen in the tires, paint protection or window etching, often listed on a second sticker beside the factory one. They are the dealer's own products, priced by the dealer. Some really are already on the car; some can be removed from the paperwork on request. This is usually the most negotiable step on the chart. We go line by line in every line on a buyer's order.

  3. Documentation fee

    Charged for preparing the paperwork. Some states limit how much a dealer may charge and some do not, and dealers often apply the same fee to every buyer. Even where the fee itself will not move, the vehicle price can move to offset it, which is another reason to negotiate the total rather than the parts.

  4. Sales tax

    The biggest added step in this example, and the one set by government rather than the dealer. How it is calculated varies: which items are taxed, whether a trade-in reduces the taxable amount, and how rebates are treated all differ by state. Here, the illustrative 6% is applied to the price plus add-ons plus doc fee ($24,194 × 0.06 = $1,451.64). Your state's revenue department is the place to confirm what applies to you.

  5. Title and registration

    State charges for recording you as the owner and putting plates on the car. They vary widely, sometimes by vehicle weight, value, age or county. The dealer collects them and passes them on. You cannot negotiate them, but you can check them: your state's motor vehicle agency publishes its fees, and the figure on your paperwork should match. Our overview of registration, title and the other state fees lists what to look up.

Notice what the chart leaves out. There is no trade-in, no rebate and no loan. Each of those can move the total, and each can also blur it: a generous trade-in figure can sit beside an inflated price, and a rebate may be applied before or after tax depending on the state. A service contract or gap product added at signing would be one more amber bar, often a large one. Keeping those out of the first conversation is what lets the waterfall stay readable.

Asking for it

The out-the-door price is the only number that lets you compare two dealers fairly, because it cannot hide anything. A lower advertised price with higher add-ons can easily finish above a higher advertised price with none. So ask for the total, in writing, before discussing payments or trade-ins. By email is ideal: it leaves a record, and it lets you ask three dealers the same question on the same afternoon.

"I'm interested in stock number ____. Could you send me the out-the-door price, itemized, including every fee, add-on and tax, assuming I register the car at my home address in ____? I'm not trading anything in, and I'll sort out financing separately."

Three things in that message do quiet work. "Itemized" means you can see each step of the waterfall rather than only its top. Naming where you register the car means the tax and state fees should be close to real. And setting the trade-in and financing aside keeps the total clean; either can be discussed afterward, as separate questions with separate answers.

Some dealers will reply with a full breakdown. Others will answer with "come in and we'll talk numbers." That is a reply too, and a useful one. A dealer who will not write down a total before you visit is unlikely to become more forthcoming once you are sitting at a desk.

When the written quote arrives

Check it against the chart. Is every step there? Are there lines you did not expect, such as a "reconditioning" or "preparation" charge on top of the price? Is the tax figure plausible for your state? Are the state fees in line with what your motor vehicle agency publishes? Any line you cannot explain is a line to ask about, and any line the dealer cannot explain is a line to ask them to remove.

Then keep the email. When you arrive to sign, the paperwork should match it. If the total on the contract is higher than the total in your inbox, the difference has a name and a reason, and you are entitled to hear both before you pick up the pen.
