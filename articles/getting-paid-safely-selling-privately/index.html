Buying & Selling

Getting paid safely when you sell your car privately

Selling a car yourself usually beats a trade-in on price. The part that goes wrong is the money, so here are the ways it can arrive, safest first.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

What does "paid" actually mean? For most of us it means a number has appeared in an account. For a seller handing over keys and a signed title, it has to mean something stronger: money that has arrived, cleared, and cannot be pulled back. A lot of private-sale trouble lives in the gap between those two meanings, because several payment methods show money arriving days before it is truly yours.

The list below ranks common methods by the risk they carry for the seller, lowest first. The meters are our editorial judgment, not measurements, and the right choice also depends on the amount, on your bank, and on who the buyer is. Banks and payment apps set their own rules for holds, limits and reversals, so check yours before the sale rather than during it.

  1. Cash, counted at the buyer's bank

    Low

    The buyer withdraws the money at a teller window while you are present, or the two of you deposit it straight into your account at your bank. Either way, a bank employee handles the notes.

    Handling: arrange it on a weekday during banking hours, and let the transaction happen inside the branch. You leave with a deposit receipt rather than an envelope.

  2. Bank wire, confirmed as received

    Low

    A wire is generally hard to reverse once it has landed. The risk is not the wire itself but a fake confirmation: a screenshot, a forwarded "receipt", or an email that looks like it came from a bank.

    Handling: only your own bank can tell you the money arrived. Log in yourself or call the number on your card. Keys move after that call, not after an email.

  3. Cashier's check, verified with the issuer

    Low-mod

    A real cashier's check is drawn on the bank's own funds. The trouble is that convincing fakes exist, and a deposited check can show as available in your account before it has actually cleared. If it later bounces, the money comes back out of your account.

    Handling: best of all, go with the buyer to their bank and watch the check being issued. Otherwise, call the issuing bank on a phone number you find yourself, never one printed on the check, and ask your own bank when the funds are final rather than merely available.

  4. Instant transfers through a bank app

    Moderate

    Fast and convenient, but daily limits are often below the price of a car, and a transfer from an account that turns out to be compromised can create a mess you did not cause.

    Handling: check both parties' limits in advance. Confirm arrival inside your own app. For larger sums, prefer a wire or a bank visit.

  5. Cash, handed over somewhere else

    Moderate

    Cash is final, which is its appeal. Away from a bank, though, you cannot easily check for counterfeits, and carrying a large sum, or being known to be carrying one, has its own risk.

    Handling: meet in a public place with cameras, bring someone with you, and deposit the money the same day. If the sum is large enough to worry about counting, it is large enough to count at a bank.

  6. Consumer payment apps and online escrow

    Elevated

    Payment apps have their own terms on disputes, holds and what counts as a protected purchase, and those terms change. Escrow services can be legitimate, but invented "escrow companies" with polished websites are a known scam pattern, usually proposed by the buyer.

    Handling: read the app's current terms for large person-to-person payments. Never use an escrow service the buyer suggests; if you want escrow, find one yourself and check it independently.

  7. Personal checks and money orders

    High

    A personal check can bounce for ordinary reasons weeks after the car has gone. Money orders are easy to counterfeit and hard to verify quickly.

    Handling: if you accept one at all, keep the car and the title until your bank confirms the funds are final. Most buyers who are serious will simply use another method.

  9. Any payment for more than the price

    Scam

    The buyer sends more than you asked, often by check, and wants the difference returned to cover a "shipper" or "agent". The payment later turns out to be fake, and the refund you sent was real. Variations include a buyer who cannot meet, will send someone to collect, and needs you to act fast.

    Handling: there is no safe version. Decline and end the conversation. The FTC describes the pattern in its guidance on fake check scams.

Rules that hold across every method

A few habits reduce the risk of almost any payment. The title is the last thing you hand over, after the money is final, not merely visible. A bill of sale, signed by both of you, records the price, the date, the time and the odometer reading; some states require one and many provide a form. Your state's motor vehicle agency explains what the seller must do after the sale, which can include removing plates and filing a notice that you no longer own the car, so that later tickets and tolls do not find their way to you. And your insurer should hear from you once the car is gone, not before.

If you still owe money on the car, the payment question changes shape, because the lender holds the title and has to be paid first. That route is mapped out in selling a car you still owe on.

Pressure is the other constant. Legitimate buyers generally accept a bank visit or a short wait for a check to clear, because they want a clean title as much as you want clean money. A buyer who resists every safe method, or needs everything done tonight, is telling you which end of the list they belong to. For the view from the other side of the table, see paying a private seller without getting burned.

The slow option, in the end, is almost always the safe one.
