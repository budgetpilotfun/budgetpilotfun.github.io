When money is tight: what to cut first

Start at the top and climb down only as far as you have to. Each rung costs more to cut than the one above it, and the bottom rung costs more than anything it could save.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

A tight month forces choices, and the easiest cut is usually whichever bill arrives next. That's how people end up keeping the satellite radio while their insurance lapses. The ladder below ranks car spending by what a cut costs you later — in money, in risk, in trouble — rather than by how much it saves today. The meter on each rung shows how much care cutting it deserves.

Cut freely
  1. Rung 1

    Comforts

    • Car washes and detailing
    • In-car subscriptions and app plans
    • Premium fuel your car doesn't require
    • Accessories

    Why here: Nothing breaks when these stop. On the fuel point, your owner's manual or the fuel door says whether premium is required or merely recommended; if it's only recommended, regular is a choice the manufacturer has already allowed. Subscriptions are worth cancelling rather than pausing if you'd forgotten you had them.

  2. Rung 2

    Optional miles

    • Trips that could be combined
    • Paid parking you could avoid with a walk
    • Toll routes where a free road costs only time

    Why here: Every mile you don't drive saves fuel and a little wear, and the saving shows up immediately. The cost is time and convenience, which in a tight month is often a fair trade. A rough cost-per-mile figure for your own car tells you what each skipped trip is worth.

  3. Rung 3

    Optional coverage

    • Rental reimbursement
    • Duplicate roadside coverage
    • Collision on a low-value, paid-off car
    • A lower deductible than you need

    Why here: These can be trimmed, but each trim moves risk onto you. Roadside help is often duplicated across a policy, a membership and a credit card, and dropping a copy costs nothing. Dropping collision on a car you own outright can make sense when the car's value is low — when dropping collision makes sense tests the idea — but if there's a loan or lease, the contract may require it. Raising a deductible saves money only if you could actually pay the higher amount.

  4. Rung 4

    Deferrable work

    • Cosmetic repairs
    • Upgrades suggested at a service visit
    • Sinking-fund contributions for the next car

    Why here: A scratch doesn't get worse if left for a month or two. A service recommendation that isn't on your owner's manual schedule can usually wait for a second opinion. Pausing the next-car fund is fine for a while. The risk on this rung is that "later" quietly becomes "never"; write down what you deferred and when you'll revisit it.

  5. Rung 5

    Negotiate, don't skip

    • The loan or lease payment
    • Insurance premium installments

    Why here: These can't simply be cut; they can only be discussed. Skipping a car payment can lead to fees, damage to your credit and, eventually, the car being taken back, on a timeline set by your contract and your state's rules. If a payment is in doubt, call the lender or insurer before the due date and ask what options exist. Whatever they offer, get it in writing. What happens if you miss a car payment sets out the usual sequence.

  6. Rung 6

    Never cut

    • Liability coverage
    • Brakes, tires, lights
    • Oil changes on schedule
    • Registration

    Why here: Each of these, cut, can cost far more than it saves. Driving without the insurance your state requires can bring fines, license trouble and, after a crash, a personal debt with no ceiling; the DMV or equivalent agency where you live can tell you what's required. Worn brakes or tires turn a small saving into a crash risk. Skipped oil changes can shorten an engine's life, which is the most expensive way to save sixty dollars. An expired registration invites tickets and, in some places, towing. If money for these runs out, the answer is to drive less or not at all until it comes back — not to drive without them.

Never cut

Using the ladder

Work down it in order and stop as soon as the month balances. Most tight months are solved on the first two rungs, which is the point of putting them first: they're painless, and they're fast. Rung three takes a phone call or two and saves money for the rest of the policy term, so it's worth doing even after the pressure passes.

Two cautions about the upper rungs. First, a cut only counts if it actually stops the spending. Cancelling an app plan that renews annually saves nothing until the renewal date, and some subscriptions bill in advance for a period you can't get back. Check when each one next charges before you count the saving. Second, beware the cut that looks like it's on rung one but belongs lower. Skipping the car wash is harmless; skipping the wash that clears winter road salt off the underside, in places that use it, may not be. The rung depends on what the spending does, not what it's called.

If you find yourself on rung five more than once or twice a year, the ladder has done its job by telling you something bigger. The car itself may cost more than your income can steadily carry, and no amount of trimming the rungs above will change that. At that point the useful question is no longer what to cut but whether this is the right car, which is a different exercise, and the car you can afford vs the car you're approved for is a good place to start it.

Copy the six rung names onto an index card and keep it with the registration in the glovebox. The order is easy to agree with now and surprisingly hard to remember at the pump in a bad week.
