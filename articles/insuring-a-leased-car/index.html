Insurance

Insuring a leased car

When you lease, the car belongs to someone else, and that someone sets conditions on how it is insured. The conditions are written in your lease agreement, not here, so treat this as a list of places to look.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Every lease agreement has an insurance section, and it is usually short and dense. Most of what it asks for falls under the nine headings below. Your agreement may ask for more, less or something different; leasing companies set their own requirements, and they are not all alike. Tick an item once you have found the matching clause in your own agreement and confirmed your policy meets it.

Nine items · the boxes are for your own use and aren't saved

  1. A lease commonly asks for liability limits above the legal minimum where you live, because the leasing company, as the car's owner, doesn't want to be the next target when an at-fault driver's coverage runs out. The required figures are written as a set of three numbers or as a single combined amount; here is how to read either.

    Check your leaseThe exact bodily-injury and property-damage figures it requires, in the notation it uses.

  2. The car is the leasing company's asset, and physical damage coverage is how it protects that asset. Expect both to be required from the first day to the day you turn the car in or buy it.

    Check your leaseWhether both coverages are named, and whether the requirement runs to turn-in or to payoff.

  3. Leases often cap the deductible on each physical damage coverage. Raising your deductible to save on premium is a reasonable move on a car you own outright; on a lease it can quietly put you out of compliance.

    Check your leaseThe maximum deductible, and whether it applies to collision and comprehensive separately.

  4. Two listings usually come up. As loss payee, the owner gets paid if the car is damaged or totaled. As additional insured, the owner is protected under your liability coverage. Your insurer adds both with the exact name and address the lease gives; a misspelled or outdated entity name is a surprisingly common snag.

    Check your leaseThe precise legal name and address to list, and which of the two roles, or both, it requires.

  5. If the car is totaled early in the lease, what the insurer pays for it can fall short of what the lease says you owe. Some leases build in a waiver that covers that shortfall; others don't, and some expect you to buy it. Buying it twice is an easy mistake. Is gap coverage already in your lease? shows which sections to search.

    Check your leaseWords like "gap waiver", "gap amount" or "early termination on total loss", and what you'd still owe.

  6. You will normally need to show coverage before you drive away, and again whenever the policy renews or changes insurers. Many insurers can send proof to a leasing company electronically once it's on the policy.

    Check your leaseWhere proof goes, and whether you must notify the lessor when you switch insurers.

  7. A lease may let the owner buy insurance on the car and charge you for it if your coverage lapses. Coverage bought that way tends to protect the owner, not you, and can cost far more than a policy you'd choose. It may also count as a default under the lease. Avoiding a single uncovered day is the whole remedy.

    Check your leaseThe default section and any clause about the lessor obtaining insurance at your expense.

  8. Some leases ask you to tell the leasing company about any accident, theft or total loss, and some have standards for how repairs are done. Damage that was properly repaired through a claim is a different matter at turn-in from damage left unrepaired, which ties this item to the inspection described in excess wear and tear.

    Check your leaseNotice requirements after an accident and any repair or parts standard.

  9. A change of address can change your premium and, across state lines, your registration and coverage requirements. Adding a household driver or driving for a delivery or rideshare app can also collide with both your policy and your lease.

    Check your leaseClauses on garaging location, permitted drivers and commercial use.

Why a lease can cost more to insure

People sometimes notice that a leased car's premium runs higher than they expected and assume leasing itself is being charged for. More often the difference comes from the checklist: limits set above what the driver would have picked, deductibles capped below what they would have picked, and physical damage coverage that cannot be dropped as the car ages. Each of those is a coverage choice made for you. Quote the same car at the same coverage and deductibles as an owned vehicle and the comparison is usually fairer than comparing against the policy you'd have chosen yourself.

Once the list is done

Put the lease's insurance section and your current declarations page side by side, and write the lease's requirement in the margin next to each matching line. Where your policy meets or beats it, you are fine. Where it falls short, call your insurer before the leasing company notices, because being told by them is always the more expensive way to find out.

Remember that the checklist has an end date. At lease end, if you return the car, the insurance requirement stops when the turn-in is complete, and the policy can come off that vehicle then, not before. If you buy the car, the leasing company comes off the policy and any new lender goes on in its place.
