Splitting car costs as a couple

One car, two people, a $920 monthly bill. There are three common ways to divide it, and each is fair by a different definition of the word.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Nobody below is real. The incomes, miles and bills were chosen so the arithmetic divides cleanly; replace them with your own and each method still works the same way.

Note that the $920 is not only the loan. Insurance, fuel and the two set-asides are there too, because a split that covers only the payment leaves the partner who happens to fill the tank or book the service quietly paying extra. Put every car cost into the pot before dividing it.

A drives to work every day; B works closer to home and uses the car mostly at weekends. Nobody's arguing yet. The question is which of three reasonable-sounding rules to adopt before somebody does.

B's share of income9.2%

Equal weight, unequal dollars. Both feel the car the same amount.

By use

Each pays in proportion to miles driven, here 75/25.

A pays$690
B pays$230
A's share of income11.5%
B's share of income5.75%

Pay for what you use. A carries twice B's burden as a share of pay.

What each column is really saying

Fifty-fifty treats the car as a shared purchase between equals, the way roommates might split a sofa. Its virtue is simplicity; nobody has to track anything or disclose a salary. Its weakness shows in the bottom rows: the same $460 is 7.7% of A's pay and 11.5% of B's. When incomes are close, that gap is small enough to ignore. When they are far apart, the lower earner quietly subsidizes the higher earner's comfort, and resentment tends to follow the math.

Proportional to income equalizes the burden instead of the bill. Both partners give up 9.2% of their take-home pay, so the car pinches both of them equally. It works well for couples who think of money as largely shared, and it adapts automatically if one income changes; recompute the percentages when it does. What it ignores is who actually uses the car. Here, A drives three times as far as B and pays only half as much again.

By use is the logic of a meter: the heavier user pays more. It suits a car that functions mainly as one person's commuting tool. But it treats every cost as if it rose with mileage, and most don't. The loan payment, the insurance premium and the registration arrive whether the car moves or sits in the driveway. Under this method B pays just 5.75% of income for a car B relies on at weekends and could not replace for $230 a month.

The fourth column nobody drew

Splitting by use breaks down because it lumps fixed and variable costs together. Separate them and a hybrid falls out naturally. Fixed costs, the $450 payment, $160 insurance and $40 registration and parking, total $650 and exist because the household owns a car; split them by income, 60/40. Variable costs, the $180 fuel and $90 maintenance, total $270 and rise with the miles; split them by use, 75/25.

That gives A $390 + $202.50 = $592.50 and B $260 + $67.50 = $327.50. Together, $920. As shares of pay, A carries 9.9% and B 8.2%: close to equal weight, tilted toward the partner who does most of the driving. It takes one more line in the spreadsheet and removes most of the arguments. Setting up a car budget spreadsheet shows how to lay the fixed and variable columns out.

Things that complicate any column

Whose name is on the loan and title. An informal split between partners doesn't change who the lender can pursue, or who legally owns the car. If one person signed alone, the other's contributions are a private arrangement. If both signed, both owe the full balance regardless of what you agreed at the kitchen table. The lender's contract governs, not the split.

The one-off bills. A $900 repair or a new set of tires will test the method more than any monthly figure. Decide in advance whether those come from the maintenance set-aside, split the same way as everything else, or get their own rule. Damage is its own case: if one partner backs into a pole, some couples treat the deductible as that person's cost, others as the household's. Either is defensible; agreeing after the fact is the hard way to find out which you believe.

Change. Jobs move, commutes shorten, babies arrive. A split that was fair in January may not be by autumn. A short review once or twice a year, with the numbers on the table, costs almost nothing. If you're weighing whether the household needs the car at all, going down to one car covers the other side of the question.

Whatever you choose, make the mechanics boring. A joint account that both partners fund by the agreed amounts on payday, with the car bills drawn from it automatically, removes the monthly settling-up that turns fair rules into friction.

No column is correct in general. The right one is the one both people can explain to each other, and would still choose if their roles were reversed.
