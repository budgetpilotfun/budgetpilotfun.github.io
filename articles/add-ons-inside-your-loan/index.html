Borrowing

The add-ons that end up inside your loan

Eight items, two prices each. The first is what the product costs. The second is what it costs once it's rolled into the loan and paid off over six years, which is the price most people actually end up paying.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Assumptions for every row: the product is added to the amount financed on a loan at 8% APR for 72 months, and paid off on schedule. Prices are illustrative round numbers we picked, not averages; real prices vary widely by seller, product and state. On these terms each dollar financed costs about $1.26 by the end; the financed figures below use the exact factor (1.26239…), and the monthly figure is what the item adds to each payment.

What the second number changes

The bars are all the same shape, and that is the point. At a given rate and term, financing marks up every item by the same proportion: here, 26.2 cents on the dollar. The $149 nitrogen and the $2,400 service contract carry the same markup. What differs is how noticeable it is. On a monthly payment, a $2.61 item disappears completely, and even the whole menu, $107.57 a month, can be made to look modest next to the car's own payment. Over six years it's $7,744.78, and $1,609.78 of that is interest on things that aren't the car.

There's a second cost the rows don't show. Every dollar added to the amount financed raises the balance against the car's value, so you owe more than the car is worth for longer. Should the car go before the loan does, the add-ons you financed are part of the shortfall.

Four habits that keep the second number small

Ask for the itemization, early. The retail installment contract lists amounts paid to others on your behalf, which is usually where these products sit. The walk-through in reading a retail installment contract marks the block. If a line is there you didn't choose, ask for it to come out before you sign, not after.

Treat "required" as a claim to verify. Contracts commonly state that optional products aren't required to get financing. If you're told otherwise, ask to see that requirement in writing from the lender.

Price the product outside the deal. Most of these can be bought elsewhere, later, or not at all. Some can be bought for cash at the dealership and kept out of the loan entirely, which removes the second number and leaves only the first.

Know the exit. Many service contracts and gap products can be cancelled, sometimes with a partial refund of the unused portion, and a refund on a financed product is often applied to the loan balance rather than paid to you. The cancellation terms are in the product's own contract. Read them before buying, so you know whether "you can always cancel" means anything.

None of this makes every add-on a mistake. A service contract on a car you plan to keep a long time, or gap coverage on a long loan with little down, can be reasonable purchases. The mistake is judging them by the first number when you'll be paying the second.
