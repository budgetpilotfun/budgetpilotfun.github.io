Running Costs

Registration, title and the other state fees

Fifteen kinds of government charge, each defined, each tagged by when it lands, and each pointing to the office that can tell you what yours will be.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

Somewhere in the paperwork of every car purchase, and again on a renewal notice each year or two, sits a cluster of government charges with names that sound interchangeable. They aren't. Each is a different kind of charge, calculated a different way, and the list in your state may include some of these and not others.

This glossary names the fee types that exist in various states and localities. It deliberately gives no amounts and doesn't say which states charge what: those figures change, differ even between counties, and are best read from the source. Each entry ends with where to confirm yours. Your state's motor vehicle agency — called the DMV, BMV, MVD or something else depending on the state — is the starting point for most of them.

One-time Recurring Triggered by an event
Title fee One-time

The charge for issuing a certificate of title — the document that records who owns the car. Paid when a car is first titled in your name, whether bought new, bought used, or brought in from another state.

Confirmyour state motor vehicle agency's fee schedule, usually under "titles."
Registration fee Recurring

The charge for permission to drive the car on public roads, renewed on a cycle your state sets. How it's calculated varies widely: a flat amount, or a figure based on the car's weight, age, value, model year, horsepower or fuel type. Some states split it into several named components on the notice.

Confirmyour renewal notice, or your motor vehicle agency's registration fee page or calculator.
License plate fee One-time

A charge for the physical plates, sometimes separate from registration and sometimes folded into it. In some places plates stay with the owner when a car is sold; in others they stay with the car. That rule decides whether you pay this again when you change cars.

Confirmthe plates section of your motor vehicle agency's site.
Specialty or personalized plate fee Recurring

An additional charge for a custom message or a special design, often renewed along with registration. Optional, but easy to forget once it is on the bill.

Confirmyour agency's specialty plate listings.
Sales or use tax on the vehicle Event

Not a fee but usually the largest government charge in a purchase. Sales tax applies to a sale; use tax generally covers a vehicle bought elsewhere and brought in for use. How a trade-in, rebates or a lease are treated varies by state, and some local jurisdictions add their own rate. On a private sale, it's often collected when you title the car rather than by the seller.

Confirmyour state revenue or taxation department, and the motor vehicle agency's page on taxes at titling.
Vehicle excise or personal property tax Recurring

In some states, cities or counties, owning a car brings a recurring tax based on its value, separate from registration. It may be billed by a local tax office rather than the motor vehicle agency, and may arrive at a different time of year — which is how it catches people out.

Confirmyour county or municipal tax assessor or treasurer.
Weight or class fee Recurring

A component of registration scaled by vehicle weight or type. Most relevant to pickups, larger SUVs and anything registered as commercial.

Confirmyour agency's registration fee tables by vehicle class.
Electric or hybrid vehicle fee Recurring

An extra registration charge that some states apply to electric or hybrid cars, generally framed as standing in for fuel tax those cars pay less of. Whether it exists, and its size, depends entirely on the state.

Confirmyour agency's registration page for alternative-fuel vehicles.
Lien recording fee Event

A charge for recording a lender's interest on the title when a car is financed. It is often paid through the purchase paperwork. The lien is released when the loan is paid off, which may or may not involve a further step on your part.

Confirmthe buyer's order or loan paperwork, and the agency's page on liens.
Title transfer fee Event

The charge for moving an existing title to a new owner. Sometimes the same as the title fee, sometimes distinct. Paid by the buyer in most transactions, but check who handles it in a private sale.

Confirmyour agency's "buying or selling a vehicle" instructions.
Inspection and emissions fees Recurring

Where a safety inspection or emissions test is required, a fee is charged for it, sometimes by the state and sometimes by a licensed private station. Requirements can differ within one state by county.

Confirmyour agency's inspection or emissions program page.
Local wheel or road tax Recurring

An add-on imposed by some counties or municipalities, often collected with state registration so it looks like part of the same fee.

Confirmthe itemization on your registration notice, or your local government's site.
Temporary tag or permit fee Event

A short-term registration that lets a car be driven before permanent plates or registration are issued.

Confirmthe dealer's paperwork or your agency's temporary permit page.
Duplicate and replacement fees Event

Charges for replacing a lost title, registration card, sticker or plate. Small, but a lost title can hold up a sale, so the time cost can exceed the fee.

Confirmyour agency's replacement documents page.
Late renewal penalty Event

A surcharge for renewing registration after the deadline, sometimes growing with the delay. The cheapest fee on this list to avoid entirely.

Confirmthe due date and grace terms printed on your renewal notice.

Not a state fee: the documentation fee

Dealers commonly charge a documentation or processing fee for preparing paperwork. It sits beside the government charges on the purchase paperwork, which makes it look official, but it is the dealer's own charge. Some states regulate or cap it; many don't. Treat it as part of the price, not as a tax. Every line on a buyer's order separates the government lines from the dealer's.

How these fees show up in a budget

The one-time fees belong in the out-the-door price of a purchase. The event-triggered ones cluster around buying, selling, financing and moving — a move across a state line, in particular, can trigger several at once, which what moving to a new state does to your car costs walks through in phases. The recurring ones are the quiet problem: they land once a year or once every two years, sometimes from two different offices, and they are exactly the kind of bill that breaks a month. A twelve-month calendar of irregular car bills shows how to spread them.

The simplest way to get your own numbers is to keep last cycle's paperwork. Before the next purchase or renewal, gather:

  • Your last registration renewal notice
  • Any local vehicle tax bill
  • Your last inspection receipt
  • The buyer's order from your last purchase
  • Your title, or your lender's name if it holds it
  • The agency's online fee calculator, if your state offers one

With those in hand, every line on this page either has a number next to it or is confirmed as not applying to you — and that short list is the complete set of state and local fees on your car.
