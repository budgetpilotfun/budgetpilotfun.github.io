Running Costs

The service menu, translated

Every item on the board, stamped with a verdict. The expensive lines are where the board and your owner's manual disagree most.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 5 min read

It hangs above the counter at plenty of quick-service shops: a lit board of numbered services and prices, with a technician walking out to recommend three of them while your car is on the lift. Some of those items are ordinary, scheduled maintenance. Some are occasionally useful. And some appear on the board far more often than they appear in any owner's manual.

The board below is a mock — no real shop's menu, and the prices are placeholders that mean nothing. What matters is the stamp on each line and the translation beneath it.

Usually needed — routine, on your manual's schedule Sometimes — worth it when an inspection shows it Check your manual — only if your schedule calls for it

Quick Service Menu

Illustrative board · placeholder prices

Oil & basics
Oil & filter change$00.00
Usually needed

Genuinely routine — but on your manual's interval and with the oil grade it specifies, not the date on the windshield sticker, which reflects the shop's habit. Many manuals list two schedules, a normal one and one for "severe" conditions such as short trips, towing or dusty roads. Read which describes your driving.

Tire rotation$00.00
Usually needed

Spreads wear across all four tires and is on most maintenance schedules. Cheap, and it protects one of the larger recurring bills. If you bought tires recently, check whether the seller includes rotation.

Fluid top-off$00.00
Usually needed

Washer fluid and a check of the rest. Often bundled with the oil change. A fluid that keeps needing topping up is a symptom to investigate, not a service to repeat.

Filters, blades & battery
Engine air filter$00.00
Sometimes

Needed at intervals, sooner in dusty places. Ask to see the old one next to a new one. On many cars it's a clip-and-lift job you can do yourself in minutes.

Cabin air filter$00.00
Sometimes

Filters the air you breathe inside the car. Legitimately due now and then; also a common add-on. The same "show me" rule applies, and so does the do-it-yourself option — the manual usually explains how to reach it.

Wiper blades$00.00
Sometimes

When they streak, chatter or leave bands, replace them. When they don't, don't. Easy to fit yourself.

Battery test / replace$00.00
Sometimes

A test is useful information, especially before cold weather. A replacement recommendation should come with the test result. Ask for the printout.

Nitrogen tire fill$00.00
Sometimes

Optional. Ordinary air is already mostly nitrogen. What keeps tires healthy is checking the pressure regularly against the placard in the driver's door jamb, whatever is inside them.

Flushes & exchanges
Transmission fluid service$000.00
Check your manual

Some manufacturers schedule it; others describe the fluid as long-life under normal use. The type of fluid matters, and the procedure differs by transmission. Follow the schedule and specification for your car, not the menu's rotation.

Coolant flush$000.00
Check your manual

Coolant does have a service life, and manuals give it — often a long one for the factory fill. The right coolant type matters as much as the timing.

Brake fluid flush$000.00
Check your manual

Brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, and many manufacturers set an interval — sometimes by years rather than miles. A real service, on a real schedule. The question is only whether it is due yet.

Power steering flush$000.00
Check your manual

Many newer cars use electric power steering and have no power steering fluid at all. If yours has hydraulic steering, the manual will say whether the fluid has an interval.

Fuel injector cleaning$000.00
Check your manual

Rarely found on a manufacturer schedule. If the car runs well, there is usually nothing to fix. If it doesn't, you want a diagnosis, not a generic treatment.

Engine flush$000.00
Check your manual

An additive run through the engine before draining the oil. Look for it in your schedule; if it isn't there, a car that has had its oil changed on time doesn't need its oil changed twice.

Reading the board in general

Three stamps cover almost everything on a menu like this, and the pattern among them is what's useful to remember. The "usually needed" items are cheap, frequent and on nearly every schedule. The "sometimes" items are real, but their timing depends on condition, so the right response is an inspection you can see. And the "check your manual" items are the expensive ones — the flushes and exchanges — which is precisely where the gap between the board and the book is widest.

The owner's manual's maintenance section, or the maintenance schedule the manufacturer publishes online, is the document to bring. It lists each service with a mileage or time interval, and it is written by the people who designed the car. Photograph that page and keep it on your phone. When a recommendation comes, the question is simply whether the schedule lists that service at this mileage — the answer settles most of it.

Where you get the work done matters less than knowing what's due, but it isn't nothing. Dealer service or independent shop scores the choice criterion by criterion. And what comes due changes with the car's age: the flushes on this board start appearing in manuals at higher mileages, which budgeting maintenance as a car ages sorts into life stages.

Three sentences that help at the counter

"Can you show me?" — for any filter, blade or worn part. A good shop will happily bring out the old filter or point at the tread.

"Is that on my manufacturer's schedule at this mileage?" — for any flush or exchange. If they don't know, you can look it up before deciding.

"Just the oil change today, thanks." — declining is always allowed. Nothing on a menu board has to be done on the day it is offered, and anything genuinely urgent can be explained in a way you can verify.

Declining an item isn't the same as ignoring it. Write down what was recommended and at what mileage, check the schedule when you're home, and book what is actually due. Over the life of a car, that one habit — letting the manual rather than the board decide — is where most of the savings on routine service come from.
