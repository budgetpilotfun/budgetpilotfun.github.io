Leasing

Paying cash for a lease buyout, or financing it

There's no general answer to this one, only conditional ones. So here are the conditions, written as rules: find the ones that describe you and follow them where they lead.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Two things are assumed throughout. First, you have already decided the buyout itself is worth doing — the car is worth at least roughly what the leasing company is asking, and you want to keep it. If you haven't settled that yet, the checklist in should you buy out your lease? comes first, and the rules below can wait. Second, "financing" means an ordinary loan on a used car, whose terms depend on the lender and on its own evaluation of you and the vehicle. Nothing here assumes any particular lender will offer you one, or at what rate.

The rules run roughly from the ones that override everything to the ones that fine-tune.

rule 01
IFthe buyout price is clearly above what the car would sell for
THENstop — the payment method isn't the question yet.

Paying cash for an overpriced car loses money slowly and invisibly; financing it loses money slowly with interest on top. Neither fixes the price. Compare against returning the car and buying something else.

rule 02
IFpaying cash would leave less in savings than your emergency cushion
THENdon't pay it all in cash. Finance some or all of it, or put part down and borrow the rest.

Interest on a car loan is a known, bounded cost. An empty account the month the water heater fails, followed by a credit card balance, is neither known nor bounded. The cushion is doing a job; don't take it off duty to save on interest.

rule 03
IFyour spare cash earns less, after tax, than a loan would cost
THENpaying cash usually wins on arithmetic.

Borrowing at a higher rate than your money earns is paying for the privilege of keeping it where it is. For scale: an illustrative $20,000 financed at 8% over 48 months is $488.26 a month and $3,436.41 in interest — a number and a rate chosen for arithmetic, not a quote. Put your own figures in and see what the interest line comes to.

rule 04
IFyour cash reliably earns more, after tax, than the loan costs
THENfinancing can make sense — but check how thin the margin is, and how "reliable" that return really is.

A savings rate can fall next month; a fixed loan rate is set in the contract. Interest you earn is generally taxable, interest you pay on a personal car loan generally is not deductible (confirm both on irs.gov or with a tax professional), so the gap has to survive both. And if the money is invested somewhere that can drop, you are comparing a guaranteed cost with a hoped-for return.

rule 05
IFthe monthly payment only fits at a long term
THENtreat that as information about the car, not the loan.

A car that has already finished a lease is not new. A long loan on it can run close to the years it has left before bigger repairs, and you can end up owing more than it's worth. If a shorter term doesn't fit the budget, a cheaper car might.

rule 06
IFyou can cover part of the price comfortably but not all
THENsplit it: pay what the cushion allows, borrow the remainder.

This isn't a compromise; it's usually the best of both. A smaller balance means less interest and less time owing more than the car is worth, while the cushion stays intact.

rule 07
IFthe car will need tires, brakes or a major service soon
THENset that money aside before deciding how much cash goes into the buyout.

During the lease, a lot of maintenance may have been included or under warranty. Once the car is yours, it all is. Spending the repair money on the buyout just moves the bill to next month, often onto a card.

rule 08
IFyou finance
THENstart with a written payoff quote and its good-through date, and line the loan up to fund before it expires.

The leasing company's figure is typically only valid until a stated date, and a loan takes time to arrange and fund. The call to your leasing company lists what to ask for, including how they accept payment from a lender.

rule 09
IFyou pay cash
THENget the leasing company's payment instructions in writing, and ask how and when the title will be released to you.

A large payment sent the wrong way is hard to recover, and a missing title makes registration in your name impossible. Confirm the instructions by calling a number you already have from your statements — not one from an email.

rule 10
IFsales tax, title or registration charges apply
THENcount them in the total before choosing, whichever way you pay.

Whether and how tax is charged on a buyout varies by state, and so does who collects it. Those charges may be financeable or may need to be paid separately — ask. Sales tax when you buy out a lease lays out the questions to put to your state.

Reading the rules together

Most people will match three or four rules at once, and they sometimes pull in different directions. When they do, the lower-numbered rule wins. A cushion (rule 02) beats a yield comparison (rules 03 and 04), because arithmetic that assumes nothing will go wrong is weaker than a plan for when something does. And rule 01 beats all of them.

If you finish the list with no clear answer — your cash earns about what a loan would cost, your cushion survives either way — then the tiebreaker is personal: some people sleep better owing nothing, some with money in the bank. Both are defensible. Pick the one you'll still be happy with on the day an unexpected bill arrives.
