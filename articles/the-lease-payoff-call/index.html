Leasing

The call to your leasing company

Before you dial, put a pen next to the phone and this page next to the pen. The call takes fifteen minutes. The notes from it will be consulted for weeks.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

Use the phone number printed on your statement or in the lessor’s app, not one from a search result. Have your account number and the car’s VIN in front of you. Then work down the log in order. Question 1 matters even if you are sure you will return the car; the payoff is how you find out what you’d be giving back.

Date / time 
Number dialed 
Rep name or ID 
Call reference no. 
Q01

“What is the payoff amount if I buy the car myself?”

the exact figure, to the cent

Every later decision compares against this number. Compare it with what similar cars sell for and you have your equity, positive or negative; lease equity, explained shows how to read the result.

Q02

“Is the figure different if a dealer or another company buys it instead of me?”

yes / no, and the other figure if yes; any restrictions on who may buy

Some lessors quote a different amount to third parties, and some limit who can purchase the car at all. If you plan to trade the car in or sell it, this decides whether that route exists.

Q03

“What date is that quote good through?”

good-through date

A payoff is a moving target. Funds that arrive after the good-through date may fall short, and a short payment can delay the title.

Q04

“If it goes past that date, how much does it change per day?”

the per-day amount, if any

This lets you work out the right figure yourself if the money is slow. With illustrative numbers: a quote of $21,850, a per-day amount of $4.10, and funds arriving six days late.

6 × $4.10 = $24.60  →  $21,850 + $24.60 = $21,874.60
Q05

“Can you itemize what’s in the payoff?”

purchase price / remaining payments / purchase option fee / other fees, each separately

A single total hides its parts. Itemized, you can check each against the purchase option terms in your contract and spot anything that doesn’t belong.

Q06

“Does the payoff include sales tax? If so, for which state, and on what amount?”

tax included yes / no; state; amount

Tax at buyout is handled differently from state to state, and who collects it varies. If the lessor collects it and your motor vehicle agency also expects it, you need to know before you pay twice. Sales tax on a lease buyout lists what to ask your state.

Q07

“How can the payment be made, and can it come from a lender on my behalf?”

accepted methods (certified check, wire, other); whether a loan check is accepted

If you’re financing the buyout, the lender will ask exactly this. Knowing it in advance saves a round of calls.

Q08

“Where does the payment go, exactly?”

payoff address or wiring details; any reference to include

Payments without the right reference can sit unapplied. And confirm any wiring instructions through the number on your statement, never through an email that arrives afterward; redirected-payment scams target exactly this moment.

Q09

“After you’re paid, how is the title released, to whom, and how long does it take?”

paper or electronic; sent to me or to my lender; expected timeframe

You generally can’t register the car in your name, or sell it, without the title or release. The timeframe tells you when to start chasing.

Q10

“Is anything else outstanding on the account?”

late fees, unpaid tolls or tickets billed to the lessor, anything pending

Charges that reached the lessor as the registered owner may be added later. Better to hear about them now than on a statement after you thought the lease was closed.

Q11

“And if I return it instead, what will the closing bill include?”

end-of-term fee amount; how wear and mileage are assessed; when the final statement is sent

The payoff only means something next to the cost of the alternative. Returning has its own price: an end-of-term charge where the agreement includes one, and whatever the inspection and odometer produce. With both paths priced, the decision is arithmetic rather than instinct.

Q12

“Can you send all of this to me in writing?”

how it will arrive (mail, email, account portal) and when

A written payoff letter is what a lender, a dealer and your own records will rely on. A number heard over the phone is only a starting point.

Before you hang up

Read your notes back to the representative: the payoff, the date, the per-day figure, the tax answer, the address. It feels awkward. It catches transposed digits, and it gives them the chance to correct a figure while you’re both still on the line. Then write the call reference number at the top of the sheet in large print, because it’s the thing you’ll need first if anything later disagrees with what you were told.

If two calls produce two different payoff figures, don’t pick the lower one and hope. Ask the lessor to reconcile them in writing. And if something doesn’t add up after that, the complaint process at consumerfinance.gov covers consumer leases.

What the sheet is for

Once the log is filled in, the first sum is a subtraction. Using the illustrative quote above and an equally illustrative market value of $24,000 for comparable cars:

$24,000 − $21,850 = $2,150 in the car’s favor

Then the adjustments: add tax if the payoff excludes it and your state charges it, add titling costs, and set the result against the return-path costs from Q11. That comparison is the real output of the call.

File the sheet with the lease agreement. The next time the account comes up, from a lender or a motor vehicle clerk or a final statement, you’ll be reading from a record instead of from memory.
