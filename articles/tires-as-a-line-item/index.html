Running Costs

Tires as a line item

The bill comes every few years; the cost accrues every mile. Here is the arithmetic that joins the two, built up one line at a time.

Budget Pilot · September 28, 2026 · 4 min read

A set of tires arrives as one bill and gets used up a mile at a time. That mismatch is why tires so often land as a surprise: the money leaves all at once, every few years, while the cost itself was accruing quietly the whole time. Turn the bill into a rate, and it becomes an ordinary line in the budget like fuel or insurance.

What follows builds that rate from the ground up for one illustrative set of tires. Every price below is a round placeholder chosen to make the arithmetic easy to follow — not a typical price, and not a quote. Tire prices vary enormously by size and type, and installation charges vary by shop. Swap in your own numbers at each step; the structure doesn't change.

Step one: the real price of the set

The price on the tire is only the first line. Everything needed to get the tires onto the car and keep them wearing evenly belongs in the total, because none of it would be spent without the tires.

The rotation count depends on the next step. At one rotation every 6,000 miles over a 40,000-mile life, they fall at 6,000, 12,000, 18,000, 24,000, 30,000 and 36,000 miles: six rotations, with the tires replaced before a seventh would be due.

Step two: how many miles you actually get

Tires carry a treadwear warranty or a mileage claim from the maker, and it is tempting to divide by that. Don't, or at least not blindly. The claim assumes conditions — regular rotation, correct inflation, an aligned car — and the useful life ends when the tread reaches the replacement depth, not when it disappears. Your own history is the better guide: how many miles did your last set last?

For this example we'll assume 40,000 usable miles. Now divide.

Cost per mile
$1,06040,000 mi = 2.65¢ per mile

That figure is the one worth carrying around. It is the true running cost of rubber on this car, and it belongs next to fuel in any honest per-mile tally.

Step three: the monthly figure

A per-mile cost becomes a monthly budget line once you know your monthly mileage. At 12,000 miles a year, or 1,000 a month, the set lasts 40 months.

Monthly cost, driving 12,000 miles a year
$1,06040 months = $26.50 a month

Check it the other way: 2.65¢ × 1,000 miles is also $26.50. Put $26.50 a month into a tire fund starting the day the set goes on, and the next set is paid for when it's needed. This is exactly the kind of irregular bill that a dedicated envelope handles well — see building a car fund, envelope by envelope.

Step four: when the calendar wins instead of the odometer

Tires age as well as wear. Rubber hardens and can crack over time even on a car that barely moves, and many tire and vehicle makers publish guidance on replacing tires past a certain age regardless of tread. That guidance is not uniform, so check what your tire maker and your owner's manual say; NHTSA's tire pages explain how to read the date code molded into the sidewall.

For a low-mileage driver, age changes the arithmetic. Here are three illustrative drivers using the same set:

12,000 miles a year

Tread runs out first, at 40 months. $26.50 a month, 2.65¢ a mile.

20,000 miles a year

40,000 miles arrives in 24 months. $1,060 ÷ 24 = $44.17 a month. Still 2.65¢ a mile.

6,000 miles a year

Suppose the set is replaced for age at six years, at 36,000 miles, after five rotations: $940 + $100 = $1,040. That is $14.44 a month but 2.89¢ a mile.

The pattern is worth noticing. For drivers limited by tread, the per-mile cost is fixed and the monthly cost follows the odometer. For drivers limited by age, the monthly cost is fixed and the per-mile cost rises, because they discard tread they never used. The six-year figure is purely an assumption for the illustration, not a recommendation.

Step five: comparing two sets honestly

Rates also settle the recurring question of whether a cheaper tire is a saving. Run a hypothetical cheaper set through the same steps: four at $120 is $480, plus the same $100 installation and $120 alignment, plus four rotations over a 30,000-mile life at $20 each — $780 in all.

The cheaper set, per mile
$78030,000 mi = 2.60¢ per mile

A $60-a-tire difference at the counter shrinks to five hundredths of a cent per mile once life is accounted for. The cheaper set also means an extra installation and alignment sooner, and a replacement bill that arrives ten months earlier for the 12,000-mile driver. Whether it's the better buy then turns on things arithmetic can't settle alone — grip, noise, wet braking, and how much the actual mileage differs from the two assumptions. If the price per mile is roughly equal, those qualities should decide.

Tires are one of several costs that come due on a slow, predictable schedule while feeling like emergencies. The car bills that arrive once a year lays out the others on a calendar.

Copy the whole method onto one line and it fits on a sticky note: (tires + installation + alignment + rotations) ÷ usable miles = cost per mile; × monthly miles = the monthly set-aside.
